## Introduction

Ask a parent what their child's soccer training looks like and they will describe a session: a pitch, a coach, a bag of balls. Ask what happens after the session and they will describe dinner, homework and bed, in the tone people use for things that do not count.

Sleep counts. It is not the gap between sessions; it is the part of training where the session turns into a better player. And unlike the session itself, sleep for young soccer players is something a parent controls more than any coach ever will. This piece is about that part of the week: why we call it training, why we will not give you a number for it, what you can change, and the one thing worth counting.

## Why sleep for young soccer players counts as training

Effort triggers adaptation. It does not produce it. The running, the touches and the decisions in a session are the stimulus; the body and the brain do the building afterwards, and much of it happens while the player is asleep. A player who trains hard and sleeps badly has done the first half of the job and skipped the half where the learning settles.

This is why a player can plateau while working harder. The effort went up and the thing that converts effort went down. Coaches see it every season and call it a plateau, or attitude, or a phase. Sometimes it is a bedtime.

So the honest position is that recovery is training in another form, and sleep is its main behaviour. It sits alongside technique, tactics and the physical work, not underneath them, and weakness in it constrains all the rest. A week with one session cut and every night protected can be a better training week than the reverse.

## Why we will not give you a number

Most articles on this subject give you a target in hours. We are not going to, and it is worth saying why.

The effects of sleep on a developing athlete are real. They are also not uniform: they vary with the player, the age, the load that week, the growth spurt they are in or not in, and things nobody can measure from the outside. Public health bodies publish ranges for adolescents and they are worth looking up, but a range is not a prescription for your child, and a number quoted with confidence in a soccer article is usually there to sound scientific rather than to help. What the evidence does support, in words, is this: a growing adolescent needs more sleep than the adults around them, and the need is larger in the years of fastest growth, which for many players are the years between thirteen and sixteen.

Two things matter more than any total. The first is regularity: a consistent bedtime across the week does more than one long sleep before a game. The second is what happens in the hour before bed, because a body that has just played a game does not switch off on its own.

## Schedules protect sleep; lectures do not

Adolescence brings a biological drift toward later sleep timing. The teenager who is not tired at ten is not being difficult; their clock has moved. School has not moved with it, and neither has the seven o'clock training slot or the early kick-off two hours' drive away.

That collision cannot be solved by telling a fifteen-year-old to be disciplined. It is solved by adults who control the schedule, and the parent is usually the adult who does. Look at the week for one specific pattern: a late finish followed by an early start. A night game and then a morning session. A tournament weekend with a long drive home and school on Monday. Those pairings are where sleep gets paid out, and they are the ones a parent can change, by declining the optional session, negotiating the pick-up time, or leaving the tournament a round early.

The rule underneath is simple to state and hard to keep: sleep does not fund optional work. If an extra session, a second club, a private coach or a late showcase can only be afforded by taking the hours out of the night, the session is the thing that goes. This is also a load question, and load has to have one owner. Club, school, self-training, travel and the second sport all add up in the same body, and if nobody is adding them up, the player is carrying a total that no single adult has seen. ## The bedroom

We take one strong position in a domain where we otherwise leave the family to decide: the phone charges outside the bedroom.

The reasons are ordinary. Light late at night disrupts the start of sleep. A feed that never ends disrupts the wind-down a tired body needs. A phone within reach disrupts sleep once it has started. What we see in players who scroll late into the night is shorter sleep and worse recovery, with comparison-driven feeds doing their own quiet damage to confidence on top. That is an observation from coaching, not a study, and it does not need a sleep tracker to confirm.

We should be honest about the tension here. We publish on social media ourselves and we want to be found there, so we are not asking players to leave it. We are asking for a relationship with screens that serves the player rather than the algorithm, and for the bedroom to be the one room that stays out of it. The family decides how to act on that; we can only say what we believe affects development.

After a game, the practical version is a wind-down: a cool shower, a light meal, screens off, and time before the light goes out. Adrenaline does not care that it is a school night.

## The one thing worth counting

If you want a number to keep, keep this one: how many nights this week the player's bedtime fell inside roughly the same hour. That is the whole count. Regularity, not perfection. Not the hours, not the wake time, not a sleep score from a wristband.

Count it for a few weeks and compare the player with themselves, not with a sibling or a teammate. Do it through conversation and a simple note, not surveillance; the point is to see the pattern, not to police it. And if the count stops changing anything, stop counting. A number that alters no decision has become a way of watching a child rather than a way of helping one.

A few weeks of counting will also show you the things worth acting on faster. Persistent tiredness, irritability, repeated colds, soreness that does not clear, and a drop in the quality of play while the effort stays high are all signs that recovery is short. Reduce the demand and look at the week. If sleep itself is the problem, with persistent difficulty falling asleep, extreme daytime sleepiness, or disturbed sleep alongside a change in mood, that is a question for a doctor, not a coach and not an article.

## The training that belongs to the day after

There is one session in the library that exists for this part of the week. [The Recovery Session (Low-Load Ball Flow)](/training/recovery-session-ball-flow) is a short, deliberately easy passage of gentle touches, mobility and unhurried wall work for the day after a game or a hard session. Its whole skill is keeping it easy. A parent cannot do it for the player, but a parent can protect the space for it, which in practice means not booking something else into the recovery day because the calendar had a gap.

## What to do this week

Three things, none of which need an announcement.

Count the nights. For one week, note whether bedtime fell inside the same hour. Say nothing about it yet.

Move the phone. Charge it in the kitchen from tonight, yours as well, because a parent who sleeps well teaches it more convincingly than any lecture.

Find one pairing. Look at the next fortnight for one late finish followed by an early start, and change one side of it.

Then hand the count over. A fourteen-year-old who keeps their own bedtime note is learning something no drill teaches: that they own the part of training nobody watches.
