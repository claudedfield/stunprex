For a coach with four players. Three players on the edges of a small square keep the ball from one defender in the middle. The two without the ball move so a pass can reach them, and the receiver chooses where the next pass goes, from where the defender is and where the free teammate stands.

## Quick start

**You need:** 4 cones, 1 ball, and a flat patch of ground.

**Set it out:**

```
            O
        +-------+
        |       |
        |   X   |     O = keeps the ball (3, on the edges)
        |       |     X = defender (1, in the middle)
        +-------+
        O       O
         about 6 m by 6 m
```

- A square about 6 m by 6 m, marked by the 4 cones. The size is a suggestion: bigger if the ball is lost on almost every pass, smaller once it rarely is.
- Three players on the edges may move along the lines; the defender starts in the middle.

**How it runs:**

1. The three keep the ball from the defender, passing along the ground.
2. The two without the ball move along the lines so a pass can reach them.
3. The receiver chooses the next pass and takes a first touch that sets it up, or plays it first time when that is on.
4. When the defender wins the ball or it goes out of the square, the defender swaps with the player who made the pass or the touch that lost it. Play starts again from the new defender's edge.

Count clean passes, not only how long the ball is kept.

**The first block:** 2 minutes of play, then 1 minute easy; four to six rounds, so every player defends at least once. These numbers are a suggestion: shorten the rounds if the defender tires.

**Load:**

- The defender's effort is a run of short presses; the round ends at about 2 minutes, and nobody stays in the middle as a punishment. No level is built on being tired.
- The defender goes for the ball, never the player.
- For 9 to 12: about 15 minutes, ending while they still want more; the first block's numbers or fewer.
- Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a movement that is breaking down; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.

**One thing to think about:** know where the next pass goes before the ball reaches you.

**Easier or harder:** easier, a bigger square or a defender who walks. Harder, see the levels below.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is a game problem for three and one. The choice it trains is the receiver's: which teammate, and with what first touch, read from where the defender is and where the free player stands. The players off the ball choose where to stand so the pass is on.

It does not train breaking a line or playing past two defenders. A rondo with four and two does that, once this one runs cleanly.

## Levels

- **Level 1:** any number of touches. Time to look, choose and pass.
- **Level 2:** at most two touches. The first touch has to set up the pass.
- **Level 3:** the defender plays at full effort, and a pass that goes past the defender through the middle of the square counts double.
- **Level 4:** first time where the picture allows; two touches otherwise. The choice has to come before the ball does.
- **Level 5:** a smaller square (about 5 m), or four keep the ball from one in a slightly bigger square, then on to four and two.

## For a parent or coach

**Watch for:**

- **Off the ball.** Do the two without the ball move to stay reachable, or stand still behind the defender?
- **The look before the ball.** Does the receiver check where the defender is before the ball arrives, or only after? If they skip it, ask after the round what they knew before the pass came.
- **The first touch.** Does it go into space, away from the defender and towards the next pass, or stop under the feet?
- **The foot.** Does the receiver use the foot the pass asks for, or turn awkwardly to avoid one?

**One question, between rounds:** "When was the ball easiest to keep, and what were the three of you doing then?"

**During play:** stay quiet while the ball is moving. When a player is stuck, a question or a short cue may help ("can the ball reach you there?"), never which pass to play.

**Praise** the movement and the choice, not the streak: "You moved and made yourself easy to find. That is the job."

**Leave for later:** the weight of every pass. First the movement, the look and the touch; the pass sharpens as those settle.

**When to simplify:** if the ball is lost on almost every pass, make the square bigger and go back to Level 1.

## Why we think it helps

Three against one is the smallest possession game with a real choice in it: two options, one defender, and enough time to look. The players off the ball learn to stand where the ball can reach them, and the receiver learns to choose before the ball arrives, because the defender closes whichever option is not ready. A pass with the weaker foot counts the same when the angle asks for it, because a player who can pass with either foot has more options in a small space. If a group keeps the ball well here and loses it in games, move to four and two, or put the same square into the start of a small-sided game.

## Self-assessment

- "Where did you stand so the ball could reach you?"
- "When did you know where the next pass was going: before the ball came, or after?"
- "Which foot did you use less today, and when could it have been the better one?"
