For a parent and one child of five to eight, in a garden, a park or a hall. The child dribbles a little trail with a story: through the forest, over two bridges, into the cave and home, touching the ball with the magic foot. There is no counting, no clock and no levels. The only thing to notice is whether the child wants to run it again.

## Quick start

**You need:** 4 to 8 markers (cones, shoes, bottles or jumpers), a ball, and a space about 10 m by 6 m.

**Set it out:**

```
                 [home]  2 markers, about 3 m apart
                    |    about 7 m
                 [cave]  2 markers, about 1 m apart
                    |
               [bridges] 2 to 4 gates, about 2.5 m wide, scattered
                    |
               [forest]  4 markers, about 1.5 m apart
               [start]
```

- About 8 to 10 m from the start to home. Every size is a suggestion; make it smaller in a small space.
- **The forest:** 4 markers in a loose line. **The bridges:** gates scattered about, not in a row. **The cave:** one pair of markers to go round. **Home:** a wide gate at the end.
- The first time, set it out together. After that, the child may set it out, and may give the places their own names.

**One run:**

1. **The forest:** dribble through the markers with the magic foot. Slow and wobbly is fine.
2. **The bridges:** cross any two, whichever look like bridges today.
3. **The cave:** dribble in, turn round the markers any way at all, and come out.
4. **Home:** roll or push the ball through the home gate. Through is the aim, not fast.
5. Walk back to the start, the magic foot still on the ball if the child likes.

**The one rule:** the ball is touched with the magic foot (the one the child uses less; at this age it has only this name). Nothing else is counted or timed.

If the child wants to go again, they go again. If they want to move a bridge or add a dragon, they do. If they want to stop, they stop.

**Load:**

- For 5 to 8: about 10 to 15 minutes, ending while they still want more; a drink and a sit-down whenever they like. Pain is never part of play.
- Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a fall that hurts; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.

**One thing to think about:** the magic foot takes the ball home.

**Easier:** wider bridges, a shorter trail, fewer markers in the forest.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is play with the ball for a young child. It gives the less-used foot many easy touches inside a story, and the child chooses the route and how to turn.

It is not the counted, levelled gauntlet for older players. The [Weak-Foot Dribbling Gauntlet](/training/weak-foot-dribbling-gauntlet) is that, from nine.

## How the trail grows

There are no levels. The trail changes as the child does.

- **Starting out:** run it as set out. The child may stop and think about the magic foot several times a run. That is fine.
- **Getting fluent:** the magic foot starts to feel ordinary in the forest and on the bridges. Make the trail more interesting rather than harder: new names, markers in new places between runs.
- **The child owns it:** the child sets it out, names the places and may invent rules. Let them lead; you watch, and now and then add to the story.
- **With a second child:** a trail each, side by side. Not a race. If they start racing anyway, start them from different places.

## For a parent or coach

**Watch for:**

- The ball touched with the magic foot, even clumsily, even slowly.
- The story. Are they in the forest, on the bridge, going home?
- Wanting to go again, which is a sign it is working.
- Changing something between runs (a bridge moved, a rule invented).

**During a run:** stay quiet and let the run finish. If the strong foot takes over, say "magic foot" once on the walk back, cheerfully, as a reminder. If it keeps happening, the trail has become too hard: wider gates, shorter trail, a simpler story.

**What not to do:** count the other foot's touches, compare runs, time them, or call it the weak foot.

**Praise** the adventure and the choices: "You found all the bridges!" "Did you invent that turn in the cave yourself?" "Which bridge will you pick next time?"

**Leave for later:** which part of the foot, which way the turn goes, speed. Any turn is right; any two bridges are right.

**If the child is stuck** on where to go next, point to the next place in the story ("where's the bridge?"), not at a cone. **If interest drops** after a few runs, change one thing: move a bridge, add a marker, rename a place.

**If another adult** wants to correct the technique or count the touches, a quiet word: at this age the thing that matters is whether the child wants to do it again tomorrow.

## Why we think it helps

A young child meets a movement many times when it is part of play, and a story makes the less-used foot part of the game rather than a correction. The trail gives that foot many easy touches with a choice in each run (which bridges, which turn), and asks for no score, so nothing about it feels like a test. We give the less-used foot many touches, because a player who can use either foot has more options; at five to eight that happens in play. If the child loves the trail and never uses the magic foot in games with friends, leave it: the games are theirs.

## Self-assessment

Asked lightly, after a run, if the child is in the mood:

- "Which bridge did you cross today?"
- "How did you get out of the cave?"
- "What should we add to the trail next time?"
