For a coach with two to four players, or a player alone for the first part. The attacker builds a small set of feints (a step-over, a scissor, a body feint, a drag-back, a fake shot, or a move of their own) against a pole, then uses them against a live defender, choosing the move that this defender's stance and weight invite, and carries the ball through a gate behind the defender. Every player attacks and defends.

## Quick start

**You need:** 4 markers, a ball each, a pole or a tall marker, and about 15 m by 10 m.

**Set it out:**

```
        [  gate  ]   2 markers, about 3 m apart
            |
            |   about 5 m
            |
           (D)   defender (a pole in Part A)
            |
            |   about 8 m
            |
           (A)   attacker starts with the ball
```

- A gate of two markers about 3 m apart. The defender (or the pole) about 5 m in front of it; the attacker starts about 8 m further back. All distances are suggestions.

**Part A, building the moves (against the pole):**

1. A carries at the pole, plays a feint, and goes past it on the side the feint opened, then through the gate.
2. A works through two or three feints, on both sides, and tries joining them (a step-over into a drag-back, a body feint into a burst), keeping the ones that feel like A's own.
3. A judges each rep by whether the move would have sent a defender the wrong way, and whether the burst after it was quick.

**Part B, using them (against a live defender):**

1. D stands where the pole was and may move as soon as A starts.
2. A carries at D, reads how D stands and where D's weight is, and chooses a feint.
3. The rep counts when A carries the ball through the gate. D wins it by touching the ball or putting it out of the area.
4. Attacker and defender swap after every two reps; with three or four, the next pair is ready behind the start.

**The first block:** Part A for about 3 minutes; then Part B, about 6 attempts each as attacker, with a walk back between; 2 minutes easy between blocks, two or three blocks in all. These numbers are a suggestion.

**Load:**

- Repeated sprints: each attempt ends in a short burst; walk back between attempts, and nobody does extra attempts as a punishment.
- Young bands (9 to 12): shorter blocks, more rest, and the defender plays the ball, never the player.
- Contact: D plays the ball, no slide tackles, no tackle from behind, no pushing.
- Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a movement that is breaking down; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.

**One thing to think about:** look at how the defender stands before you choose the move.

**Easier or harder:** easier, D starts at half pace and only from the front. Harder, see the levels below.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

The choice this drill trains is which feint to use against this defender, and when; the task fixes the gate and the defender and leaves the move, the side and the moment open. Part A builds the moves; Part B is where the choice is made, against someone who can choose too.

It does not train the choice between carrying past and passing. [Beat and Decide](/training/beat-and-decide-1v1) trains that.

## Levels

- **Level 1:** Part A only. Two or three feints against the pole, both sides, done cleanly and with a burst after them.
- **Level 2:** Part A with more feints and A's own joins. A builds the set.
- **Level 3:** Part B with D at half pace. A reads D's stance and picks a move.
- **Level 4:** Part B with D fully live. A sells the move and commits to it.
- **Level 5:** Part B in a narrower area (about 6 m wide), and D may start from the side as well as the front. A has less time and space, and the stance to read changes each rep.

## For a parent or coach

**Watch for:**

- **The read.** Does A look at how D stands before choosing, or play the same move every time?
- **The sell.** Does D move with the feint, or ignore it? If D does not move, the feint was not sold.
- **The burst.** After the feint, does A go at once, or slow down and let D recover?
- **The sides.** Does A use both sides, or always come back to one?
- **The set.** After a few reps, is A's set of moves growing, and do they look like A's own?

**One question, between reps:** "How was D standing, and why did you choose that move?"

**During a rep:** stay quiet while the ball is moving. When a player is stuck, a question or a short cue may help ("how is D standing?"), never which move to use.

**Praise** the choice and the commitment, whatever the result: "You saw D's weight was on the left, sold it that way and went right. That was your read."

**Leave for later:** naming which feint to use. The player builds the set and chooses; coach the read, the sell and the burst, not a fixed move.

**When to simplify:** if A loses the ball on almost every attempt, go back to Level 3 with D at half pace until A gets through again.

## Why we think it helps

A defender who moves first gives the attacker the space, and a feint is a way to make the defender move. This drill builds a player's own set of feints, then asks the player to choose from it against a live defender, so the move comes from what the defender is doing, not from a rehearsed order. A failed attempt shows what to change: too slow, not sold, or the wrong move for that stance. Working on both sides trains the foot a player uses less with deliberate practice, because a player who can go either way has more options. If a player beats defenders here and not in games, put one against one moments into a small-sided game and see whether the read of the defender's stance is still there.

## Self-assessment

- "Which feint is becoming yours, and which still needs work?"
- "How did you decide which move to use?"
- "When a move failed, what did it tell you to change?"
