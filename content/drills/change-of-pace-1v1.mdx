For a coach with two to four players. One player takes the ball at a defender slowly, watches how the defender stands, and changes pace past them at the moment they choose.

## Quick start

**You need:** 6 markers, several balls, and a space about 12 m wide and 15 m long.

**Set it out:**

```
        [G]  target gate, 2 m wide, on the end line
            about 6 m
        (D) defender
            about 6 m
        (A) attacker with the ball, on the start line
   grid about 12 m wide and 15 m long
```

- Four markers for the corners of a grid about 12 m wide and 15 m long, and two for a gate about 2 m wide in the middle of the far end. D starts about 6 m from A, between A and the gate. All sizes are suggestions.

**One rep:**

1. A goes when ready and walks or jogs the ball at D. D comes to meet A and defends the gate.
2. A watches D's feet and weight and chooses the moment and the side to change pace.
3. A accelerates past D and dribbles through the gate.
4. The rep ends when A goes through the gate, D wins the ball, or the ball leaves the grid.
5. A and D walk back and swap roles.

**With three:** the third player waits at the start and comes in as the next attacker; the defender rotates out. **With four:** two pairs take turns, so each pair rests while the other plays.

**The first block:** 5 attempts each as attacker, then 3 minutes easy (passing, walking, a drink); two or three blocks in all. These numbers are a suggestion: use fewer if the bursts get slower.

**Load:**

- Each change of pace is a short burst at full effort. The walk back and the other pair's turn come before the next one. Stop when the bursts get slower and sloppier, and never use extra reps as a punishment.
- Pair players by size and maturity, not by age or level alone. D plays for the ball, never into the back or across the legs. Any knock to the head stops play for that player.
- Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a movement that is breaking down; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.

**One thing to think about:** what is the defender doing at the moment you go?

**Easier or harder:** easier, D only shadows and does not tackle. Harder, see the levels below.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is a game problem with one attacker and one defender. The choice the player makes is when to change pace and to which side, read from how the defender stands.

It does not train a carry with a defender recovering from behind, or a finish. [Outrun the Recovery](/training/carry-under-recovery-pressure) trains the carry with a defender alongside.

## Levels

- **Level 1:** D shadows and does not tackle. A finds the contrast: a slow approach, then a sudden change, with a low, balanced start to the burst.
- **Level 2:** D defends at full effort, and A may go past on one side only, chosen before the rep. The pace change has to do the work, not the angle.
- **Level 3:** A may go either side and chooses it from D's weight.
- **Level 4:** D may show a side or step in early on purpose. A reads whether the opening is real before going.
- **Level 5:** a smaller grid, and a second defender starts behind the gate and comes out once A passes the first. A has to change pace and get through before the second arrives. The contact line above holds for both defenders.

## For a parent or coach

**Watch for:**

- **The contrast.** Is there a real slow, then a real fast, or one speed all the way?
- **The moment.** Does A go when D is set or leaning, or at a moment D can answer?
- **The start of the burst.** Sudden and balanced, or a stumble that gives D time?
- **Both sides.** At Level 3 and above, does the side change with D's weight, or is it the same side every time?

**One question, between reps:** "What was the defender doing when you went?"

**During a rep:** stay quiet. When a player is stuck, a question or a short cue may help ("what are their feet doing?"), never when to go or which side.

**Praise** the read and the change, whatever the result: "You waited until they stopped, then went. Even with the tackle, that was the right moment."

**Leave for later:** top speed. First the contrast and the moment; a well-timed change at three-quarter pace gets past more often than a badly timed one at full pace.

**When to simplify:** if A runs at one speed rep after rep, go back to Level 1 and ask the question above after each rep.

## Why we think it helps

Many 1v1s in a game are won without a trick, by a change of speed at the right moment. This drill gives the attacker a defender who reacts, so the moment has to be read, not guessed. We judge a rep by the read and the change: a well-timed burst that gets tackled counts for more than a lucky one that gets through. A failed rep is worth one question about what broke (the approach, the moment, or the start of the burst). If the player changes pace well here and still runs at one speed in games, put the same picture into a small-sided game where a dribble through a gate scores.

## Self-assessment

- "When did your change of pace work best? What was the defender doing?"
- "Did you get past without a trick? How did that feel?"
- "Which part is hardest for you: the slow approach, the read, or the burst?"
