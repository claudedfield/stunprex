## Quick start

**You need:** a notebook or a notes app, a watch, and whatever the drill you choose needs. You do this alone. The first time takes about 20 minutes.

**The four steps of your first session:**

1. **Name it (3 minutes).** Write down one thing in your game that you want to work on. Make it something you could see on a video. "Get better" is too wide. "My first touch runs away from me when the pass is firm" is the right size.
2. **Choose the work (2 minutes).** Pick one drill that fits it, from [the drill library](/training) or from what you already know. Write its name, and one sign that would tell you a repetition was good.
3. **Do it (about 12 minutes).** Run the first block of that drill as its page describes it. Follow what the page says about how long and how hard, and do not go beyond it. If the page does not say, or the drill is your own, keep the work to about 12 minutes at a pace at which you could still talk.
4. **Write it down (3 minutes).** Three lines about the work, not about a result: what went better than you expected, what did not, and what you will change next time.

**An example.** Named: "My first touch runs away from me when the pass is firm." Chosen: [Wall Scan-Receive](/training/wall-scan-receive), with the sign "the ball stops within a step of the marker". Done: its first block, 4 minutes with each foot. Written: "Right foot fine. Left foot ran away about half the time. Next time I start with the left."

**When it goes wrong:**

- You cannot think of one thing: take the last mistake you remember from a game or a training session, and start there.
- The drill you chose turns out not to fit: finish the block anyway, and write that down as your "what I will change".
- You run out of time: stop the work and still write the three lines. The session is not finished until they are written.

**One thing to think about:** is it something you really struggle with, or something you are already good at?

**Easier or harder:** easier is to let a coach or a parent help you with step 1 only, by asking you questions. Harder is to come back to the same thing a week later and read your last entry before you start.

Stop the work if something hurts.

## What this session is, and what it is not

This is a supporting session for practising alone. It has no football task of its own. The football comes from the drill you choose, and that drill's page sets the space and the equipment. Where it also says how long and how hard, follow it. Where it does not, the 12 minutes at a talking pace from the quick start is your limit.

What you practise here is choosing your own work: what to work on, what to do about it, and how to tell afterwards how it went. Those choices are yours. The four steps are the only fixed part.

It does not replace a coach, and it does not train anything by itself. If the drill you chose is a poor fit, the session may show you that. It will not fix it for you.

## The four steps, a little further

- **Name it.** Pick one thing only. If it helps, finish this sentence: "In games I keep ..." The more exactly you can say when it happens, the easier the next step becomes.
- **Choose the work.** One drill is enough. Ask yourself whether it really works on the thing you named, or whether you picked it because you like it.
- **Do it.** Give it the attention you would give it with a coach watching.
- **Write it down.** Keep the three lines. Before your next session on the same thing, read the last entry first.

Compare your entries only with your own earlier ones. They say nothing about anybody else.

## What good looks like

- The thing you named is specific, and it is not one of your strengths.
- The drill you chose has a clear link to it. You can say in one sentence why you chose it.
- The three lines say what happened. They do not say how you feel about yourself as a player.
- Your next session on the same thing starts from the last entry.

## Ways to change it

These follow one another over weeks, not within one session.

- **The same thing again (the next step).** Stay with one thing for three or four sessions before you name a new one. Read your last entry each time. Move on when your entries stop finding anything to change. If three or four entries say the same thing did not get better, change the drill before you change the thing.
- **A sharper name (harder).** Rewrite what you named so that it says when and where it happens: "when the ball comes from my left", "in the last ten minutes". Go back to the wider name if you cannot find a drill for the sharper one.
- **Your own drill (harder).** Change one thing in the drill you chose so that it fits better: a smaller target, the other foot, less time. Write down what you changed and why. Go back to the page's version if your change makes it something else.
- **A log per thing (different).** Keep one page for each thing you work on, so you can read down it. Use this once you have worked on two or three things.
- **Without the page (the check).** In a week when nobody sets your practice, see whether you still name one thing, choose work for it and write the three lines.

## For a coach or parent

This session belongs to the player. Your part is small.

**You can ask:**

- "What did you name, and why that?"
- "How does the drill you chose work on it?"
- "What did your last entry say?"

**Leave alone:** the choice itself. If you think the player named the wrong thing or chose the wrong drill, ask about it afterwards and let the next entry answer. Do not rewrite their plan.

**Make it easier, or stop, when:**

- the player cannot get started: sit with them for step 1 and ask questions until one thing is on the page;
- every session is about something they already do well: ask what they left off the list;
- the notebook turns into a list of faults: that is not what it is for. Go back to three lines about one session;
- something hurts in the work: stop.

## Why we think it helps

This is our reasoning, and we have not tested it. A coach is not always there. A player who can name what to work on and choose work for it has something to do with that time. Writing three lines afterwards gives the next session somewhere to start.

**Where it should show later:** in a week with no coach and no set practice, you still name one thing, choose work that fits it, and write down how it went.

**What would count against it:** the notebook has entries only when somebody asks for them. Or every entry is about something you were already good at.
