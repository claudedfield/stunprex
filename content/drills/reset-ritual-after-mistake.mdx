## Quick start

**You need:** a ball, a wall, six cones or markers and a watch. Flat ground about 12 m out from the wall and 4 m wide (14 m if you move the box back, see below). You can do this alone.

**Set it out:**

```
   ███████████ WALL ███████████
            •   1 m   •          ← the target: two markers at the foot of the wall
                 ↑
                8 m
                 ↓
            •         •
              [you]              ← your box: 2 m × 2 m
            •         •
```

- **The target:** two markers at the foot of the wall, 1 m apart.
- **Your box:** four markers in a square of 2 m × 2 m, its front edge 8 m from the wall.

**First, build your reset.** Pick three things and keep them the same every time:

1. one slow breath out;
2. your own signal: a clap, a touch of the chest, or one word such as "next";
3. eyes up, onto the target.

Run through it five times without the ball.

**One repetition:**

1. From inside the box, pass the ball into the wall, anywhere on it.
2. Control the ball as it comes back, with one touch, so that it stays inside the box.
3. With your second touch, pass it along the ground between the two target markers.

**A hit** is a ball that reaches the wall between the markers. **Anything else is a miss:** the first touch leaves the box, you need a third touch, or the pass goes wide.

**After every miss:** run your reset before you do anything else. Breathe out, give your signal, eyes up. Then fetch the ball if you need to and start the next repetition. After a hit, stop the ball in the box as it comes back and start again from step 1.

**The first block:** 3 minutes at a calm pace, then 1 minute rest. Stop if something hurts, or if you are only getting angrier.

**One thing to think about:** the repetition after a miss. That is the one this drill is for.

**Easier or harder:** you should miss often and also hit often. If you hit almost nothing, put the target markers 1.5 m apart, or move your box to 6 m. If you hit almost everything, put them 70 cm apart, or use your weaker foot.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is a supporting drill, for practising alone or with one partner. The football task is only there to make you miss. What you are practising is what you do in the few seconds after a mistake.

There is no opponent and no decision in it. It is also a training habit and nothing more. It is not a remedy if football is making you very upset, or if the upset stays with you after the session. Then talk to a parent or another adult you trust; you do not have to wait until it happens often. A doctor or a school counsellor can help too.

## A full session

- Three blocks of 3 minutes with 1 minute rest between them. About 12 minutes.
- Change the foot each block.
- In each rest, ask yourself one thing: after my misses in that block, how was the next repetition? You do not need to count anything.
- Keep the task hard enough that the misses keep coming. A block with no misses gives the reset nothing to do, so make the target narrower.
- **With a partner:** the partner stands next to the wall, two steps to the side of the target, and passes the ball to you a little awkwardly: sometimes firm, sometimes bouncing. You control it in the box and pass at the target. The partner collects the ball as it comes off the wall, or fetches it after a miss while you run your reset, and feeds again. Swap after each block. The partner says nothing about your misses.

## What good looks like

- The reset comes straight after the miss, and it is the same three things every time.
- The repetition after a miss is a real attempt. You are looking at the target, and you are not rushing to make up for the last one.
- One miss stays one miss. It does not turn into three hurried ones.
- You do not skip the reset when you are annoyed. That is when you need it.

## Ways to change it

- **More misses (harder).** Narrow the target to 70 cm, or move your box to 10 m; you then need about 14 m of ground. The reset gets more work. Go back if you no longer hit anything.
- **The next one counts (harder).** After every miss, the next repetition is the one you judge: was it a clean attempt? Say "yes" or "no" to yourself and carry on. Drop this if it makes you tight.
- **Against the watch (harder).** Count your hits in 2 minutes and try to match that number in the next 2 minutes. You still run the full reset after every miss, even though it costs time. Drop the watch if you start skipping the reset.
- **With someone watching (harder).** A partner or a parent watches one block and says nothing, then and afterwards. Use this only if you want it. End it for today if your play drops sharply while you are watched.
- **In a small game (the check).** Play any small-sided game. The game comes first: if you lose the ball, react as you normally would, chase or get back. Run the reset at the first moment the game allows, and keep it to a second: breath, signal, eyes up. A coach or a friend watches one thing: what you do with your next involvement.

## For a parent or coach

**Watch for:**

- whether the reset happens after a miss, or the player goes straight into a rushed repetition;
- whether the repetition after a miss looks like the ones before it;
- a reset that is performed but empty: the signal is given, and the eyes are still on the ground.

**Things to ask:**

- "What exactly is your reset?"
- "After your worst miss today, how was the next one?"

**Leave alone:** the misses themselves. In this drill they are meant to happen. Do not coach the technique of the pass here.

**Make it easier, or stop, when:**

- the task or the routine confuses the player: go back to the three steps without the ball;
- nothing is being hit at all: widen the target or move the box closer, so there are hits to come back to;
- frustration takes over and the reset has stopped helping: stop for today. Do not keep a player in this drill to toughen them up;
- something hurts: stop.

If the player is very upset by football, in or out of this drill, that is a conversation and not a drill.

## Why we think it helps

This is our reasoning, and we have not tested it. A mistake can cost you more than the one ball. Sometimes the head goes down, the next action is hurried, and one error turns into two. A short routine gives you something to do in that moment other than replaying the miss.

We do not know whether a routine practised at a wall will be there in a match. That is why the small-game version is the check.

**Where it should show later:** in a small game or a match, after you lose the ball or misplace a pass. You react first, run your reset when the game lets you, and your next involvement is a normal one: you show for the ball again and play what is on.

**What would count against it:** the routine is there at the wall and gone in games. Or it is there in games and your next involvement is still hurried or hidden.
