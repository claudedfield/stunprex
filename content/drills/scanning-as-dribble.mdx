For a player of nine and over, alone or with up to three others, each with a ball. Six gates stand scattered in a small field. The player carries the ball through the field and, before reaching a gate, says its name out loud, then goes through it. To say a gate's name the player has to see it, and to see it while the ball is moving the player has to look up. Which gate comes next is always the player's choice.

## Quick start

**You need:** a ball for each player, 12 markers (cones, shoes, bottles or jumpers) and about 15 m by 15 m of ground (12 m by 12 m works; 10 m by 10 m with the gates about 3 m apart and the name said from about 2 m).

**Set it out:**

```
   [orange far]                [blue far]
     .  .                        .  .
                 [brown near]
                    .  .
   [blue near]             (home)
     .  .
                                 [orange near]
                [brown far]          .  .
                   .  .

        <------- about 15 m by 15 m ------->
```

- Six gates, two markers each, about 1.5 m wide, scattered at mixed angles and not in a circuit. At least 4 m between gates, so saying a gate's name from about 3 m away needs a real look.
- Three pairs you can tell apart (colours, or pairs of objects: shoes, bottles, jumpers). The player names each gate during set-up by its colour and a word of their own ("orange far", "blue near"); the names stay for the session.
- A home square of about 2 m in the middle: the start and the end of each run.
- The player sets out the field. Between blocks the player moves the gates to new places, so the next block's field has to be found again by looking.
- All the sizes and times are suggestions.

**One run (about 60 seconds):**

1. Dribble out of the home square in any direction.
2. While the ball is moving, look up, choose the next gate and say its name before you are about 3 m from it.
3. Carry through that gate. Then choose the next one. Not the same gate twice in a row, and not the same colour twice in a row.
4. A gate entered without saying its name first, or after stopping the ball to look, is a "blind entry". The player notices it, says "blind" and carries on. Nothing else happens.
5. After about 60 seconds, back to home. If the player wants to, they note two things: how many gates, and how many blind entries.

**One session:** three blocks of four runs. In each block the first two runs favour one foot and the next two the other; both feet are always allowed. Between blocks, about 90 seconds: a drink, and the player moves the gates.

**Load:**

- Runs of about 60 seconds, four to a block, about 90 seconds between blocks, three blocks: about 15 to 18 minutes. For 9 to 12: end while they still want more, and no level built on being tired; pain is never part of training.
- With more than one player in the field: everyone carries with the head up, and a player who sees a crash coming slows down; the field gets bigger with each extra player.
- Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a fall that hurts; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.

**One thing to think about:** where is your next gate, and when did you last see it?

**Easier or harder:** easier, fewer gates, closer together, at walking pace. Harder, see the levels below.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

The choice this drill trains is which gate next, made from what the player sees while the ball is moving. The task fixes the gates and the rule that a gate is named before it is entered; it leaves the route, the order and the touch to the player.

It is not a test and not a race against anyone. Blind entries are not mistakes to be punished; they show the player when the eyes went down. A player who keeps a note compares it only with their own earlier notes.

It trains close control and the look while carrying. It does not train reading an opponent: the gates do not move by themselves.

## Levels

- **Level 1:** as in the quick start. The player moves the gates between blocks.
- **Level 2:** the player names two gates at once ("brown far, then blue near!") and goes through both in that order, then names the next two.
- **Level 3:** the next gate's name comes within three touches of leaving the last one. Each block changes one thing: a different ball (a futsal ball or a smaller one), a different surface, or one block carried with the other foot only.
- **Level 4:** a helper stands outside the field with bibs in the gate colours. Now and then, without a word, the helper holds up a bib: while it is up, that colour's gates are shut. The player finds the bib by looking up and chooses among the open gates. A gate already named that turns out to be shut is dropped, and the player names another.
- **Level 5:** before each run the player draws or chooses one card: outside of the foot only; three gates named at once; count blind entries silently and say the number at the end. The player may time gates per run against their own earlier runs only.

## In a field of cones

The same carry through a crowded space: a field of many cones makes close control, the look and the route harder. This part needs an adult or a helper outside the field.

- **Set out:** a square of about 10 m by 10 m, marked at the corners (4 cones). Inside it, 15 to 18 cones scattered at random: dense, but with a way through in every direction and no straight path. A gate of two cones, about 1 m wide, in the middle of three sides (6 cones); the fourth side is the way in. 25 to 28 cones in all.
- **Run:** the player dribbles freely through the field. Now and then (every 8 to 20 seconds), the adult outside the field names a gate: "A!". The player finds a route to it, goes through, comes back in through the same gate and carries on. The choice here is the route; the call fixes the gate.
- **Rounds:** about 3 minutes, about 90 seconds rest, three rounds. Between rounds, move 4 or 5 cones.
- **More players:** two or three in the field at once, each with a ball; the adult names one player and a gate at a time. The other players become moving things to look out for.
- **Harder:** a smaller square (about 8 m by 8 m), a fourth gate, or two gates named at once ("A, then C").

## Indoors

The same drill on a hall floor, where the ball rolls faster and further.

- Flat disc markers, which the ball glances off less than tall cones. Gates about 1.2 to 1.5 m wide. A futsal ball if you have one.
- The gate is named from about 2.5 m on a hall floor; further is fine.
- Keep every gate at least 1 m from a wall. A gate that can only be reached by running at a wall is not a gate.
- After a ball runs away, one question, between runs: "what kind of touch would have kept the ball?" The player finds the answer.

For children of five to eight, the same field becomes a story game: see [Safari Spotter](/training/safari-spotter).

## For a parent or coach

The drill runs without an adult. Where one is present:

**Watch for:**

- The look before the name. Is the name the result of a look, or the layout remembered? A moved field shows it.
- The touch under the look: long enough to free the eyes, short enough to keep the ball.
- When the name comes: at the gate, or early, with the eyes moving again between gates.
- After a ball runs away: does the player look up again, or go back to watching the ball?
- An honest "blind", including the ones nobody else would have seen.

**During a run:** stay quiet. Between runs, a question may help ("which gate didn't you visit?"), never which gate to go to.

**Praise** the look and the choice: "You said that one from five metres with the ball moving." "Your head came up as soon as you were through."

**Leave for later:** the pace in the first sessions, balls that run away when the head comes up, which gates the player favours.

**If the player stops the ball to look:** between runs, ask: "Can you make the same look with the ball rolling?"

**If the player always uses the same few gates:** between runs: "Which gate haven't you named yet? Start with it."

**What not to do:** count for the player, compare their numbers with another player's, or call the next gate in the gate field. In the field of cones the adult's call sets where to go, never the way there.

## Why we think it helps

A player who has to name the next gate before reaching it has to look up while the ball is moving, because the rule needs it, not because an adult said "head up". Moving the gates between blocks means the look has to find the field again rather than remember it. We train the weaker foot with deliberate practice, because a player who can use either foot has more options. If the player does well here and still carries with the head down in games, move to the field of cones with other players in it, and then to games where the things to look for move.

## Self-assessment

At the end of the session:

- "When did your head come up: before the gate, or at it?"
- "Which touch let you look up and still keep the ball?"
- "What would you change in the field next time?"
