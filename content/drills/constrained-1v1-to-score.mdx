For a coach with two or three players. One attacker, one defender, one small goal and about eight seconds. The attacker looks at the defender, chooses how to beat them, and tries to score; the defender tries to win the ball. Every player attacks and defends, and a rep that goes wrong is a picture to look at, not a verdict.

## Quick start

**You need:** 4 corner cones, 2 cones for the goal, 1 ball, and a flat patch about 10 m by 8 m.

**Set it out:**

```
          [goal, about 2 m]
            (D)  about 1 m in front of the goal
             |
             |   about 10 m
             |
            (A)  with the ball
      +----- about 8 m -----+
```

- A rectangle about 10 m long and 8 m wide. The goal is 2 cones about 2 m apart in the middle of the far line. The sizes are suggestions.
- The defender starts about 1 m in front of the goal, facing the attacker. The attacker starts at the near end, in the middle, with the ball.

**One rep:**

1. The attacker looks at the goal, the space and the defender, and goes when ready.
2. The attacker tries to beat the defender and score through the goal; the defender steps up and tries to win the ball.
3. The rep ends on a goal (the attacker's point), a ball won under control (the defender's point), the ball out of the rectangle, or about 8 seconds passing (no point). The coach, or the third player, keeps the time and says "time" only when it has run out.
4. Reset. The next rep starts when both are ready.

**Rotation:** each player attacks for a full block, then the roles swap. With three, one attacks, one defends and one keeps the time; rotate every 4 reps.

**The first block:** 3 minutes attacking, then 90 seconds rest; swap; two or three blocks each. These numbers are a suggestion: shorten the blocks if the duels get slower.

**The score:** a quiet tally gives each rep a stake. Nobody is announced the winner at the end; after the block, talk about moments from both players, not about who had more points.

**Load:**

- Pair players by size and maturity, not by age or level alone; contact is for the ball, from the side or the front, never from behind; the bout ends at about 8 seconds, and the rest above comes between blocks. No level is built on being tired.
- For 9 to 12: about 15 minutes, ending while they still want more; the first block's numbers or fewer.
- Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a movement that is breaking down; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.

**One thing to think about:** look at the defender before you choose.

**Easier or harder:** easier, a wider goal or a defender who only shadows. Harder, see the levels below.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is a game problem with one attacker and one defender. The choice it trains is the attacker's: which way, with which move, and when, read from the defender's stance and weight. The time limit asks for a choice; it does not say which.

It does not train the choice between beating a player and passing. [Beat and Decide](/training/beat-and-decide-1v1) trains that.

## Levels

- **Level 1:** as set out, about 8 seconds. The defender does not tackle from behind.
- **Level 2:** about 6 seconds. The choice has to come earlier.
- **Level 3:** a smaller rectangle, about 8 m by 6 m. Less room around the defender; small feints matter more than speed.
- **Level 4:** before each rep the attacker draws a card (finish with the weaker foot, start facing away from the goal and turn first, or at most two touches before the shot). The attacker holds the card's rule and still reads the defender.
- **Level 5:** a second defender starts about 3 m behind the first and may join once the attacker is past the first. The attacker reads both.

## In a hall

The same game runs indoors. Conditions, not a separate drill:

- **Space:** about 8 m by 6 m, and about 7 seconds.
- **Ball:** a futsal ball (size 4, low-bounce), or a standard ball with a little air let out; never a fully pumped standard ball at this range indoors.
- **Goal:** low, flat cones about 2 m apart (tall cones trip on a hard floor); or, where a clear wall is free, a section about 2 m wide marked low on the wall with tape, a goal below knee height. With the wall goal, a ball that comes back off the wall stays in play until the time runs out.
- **Floor:** indoor shoes with flat soles; a dry floor; walls and benches clear of the run-off. The hall's own rules come first.
- **Watch for:** a touch that runs away on the faster floor, and feints that shrink so the foot does not slip.

## For a parent or coach

**Watch for:**

- **The look.** Does the attacker look at the defender before going, or set off with a move chosen in advance?
- **The choice.** A clear choice in the first seconds, or a slow dribble waiting for a mistake?
- **The feint.** Any attempt to move the defender's weight (a shoulder drop, a pause, a cut), or the same pace and line every time?
- **The sides.** Does the attacker go both ways, or always to one side?
- **After a failed rep.** Head down and a slow reset, or straight back in, curious? The game should feel like a problem to solve.
- **The defender.** Patient and reading, or diving in early?

**One question, between reps:** "What did you see in the defender, and what did you choose?"

**During a rep:** stay quiet while the ball is moving. When a player is stuck, a question or a short cue may help between reps ("what were the defender's feet doing?"), never which way to go.

**After a rep that went wrong:** one question, in the tone of curiosity, then back to the game. If they hesitated: "What were you waiting for?" If they committed and lost it: "You went for it. What would you try next time?" If the touch ran away: "Which part of the foot next time?"

**Praise** the choice and the commitment, whatever the result: "You saw the defender lean and went the other way. That was your read." To the defender: "You waited for the touch."

**Leave for later:** the technique of each feint. Let the player invent before refining.

**When to simplify:** if the attacker loses the ball on almost every rep, widen the goal or go back to a defender who only shadows.

## Why we think it helps

One against one strips a match moment to its core: a player, an opponent, a goal and little time. A player who has not decided tends to dribble slowly and wait, and a player with one rehearsed move tends to use it whatever the defender does. Here the defender changes each rep and the time asks for a choice, so the attacker has to read and commit. Many short reps with a calm adult also make a failed attempt ordinary, which is how we think composure is built: as a skill trained in many small moments. If a player is brave here and not in games, put one against one moments into a small-sided game and see whether the look at the defender is still there. The weaker-foot card is there because a player who can finish with either foot has more options.

## Self-assessment

- "What did you see in the defender on your best rep?"
- "When did you choose your move: before you set off, or once you saw the defender?"
- "After a rep that went wrong, what did you change on the next one?"
