For a coach with six to ten players from nine. Two small teams play to mini-goals on a pitch split into three zones, one player of each team starting in each. At each change, every player moves forward one zone, and the forward goes back to defend. By the end of the session, every player has defended, built the play and attacked, and nobody has been told which role is theirs.

## Quick start

**You need:** cones for the pitch and the zone lines, 2 mini-goals, bibs in 2 colours, 2 or 3 spare balls.

**Set it out:**

```
            mini-goal A
        +---------------+
        |    ATTACK     |   about 10 m deep
        +---------------+
        |   MIDFIELD    |   about 10 m deep
        +---------------+
        |    DEFEND     |   about 10 m deep
        +---------------+
            mini-goal B
   3v3: about 25 to 30 m by 20 m. Zones shown for one team; the other team's run the other way.
```

- **3v3:** a pitch about 25 to 30 m long and 20 m wide, cut into three zones across its length. Each team starts one player in each zone: its defender in the zone by its own goal, its forward in the zone by the other goal.
- **4v4 (13 and over, or a group of eight):** a pitch about 30 to 40 m by 25 m, the same three zones. Each team starts two players in midfield. The four places go round in a fixed order: defend, first midfield, second midfield, attack, then back to defend; name the order before the first segment.
- **5v5:** one spare player per team waits at the side and comes in at the defender's place at each change; the forward goes out. With an odd number, the spare joins one team and goes round with it the same way.
- All the sizes and times are suggestions.

**One session:**

1. **First segment.** Play a normal small-sided game. The zones are only where players start; everyone may go anywhere. The adult watches and says nothing about how anyone plays the role.
2. **The change.** The adult says "change". Play stops where the ball is; every player moves forward one zone (the forward goes back to defend); play restarts from that spot with the team that had the ball.
3. **Second and third segments.** The same game, everyone in a new role. After the third, every player has played every zone once (four segments in 4v4).
4. **Close.** Stop the game; the players talk in pairs about the questions under Self-assessment, not in front of the group.

**Load:**

- Segments of about 3 minutes for 9 to 12, about 4 to 5 minutes from 13; a drink and about 2 minutes easy after each full circle; one or two circles in a session. For 9 to 12: about 20 minutes in all, ending while they still want more, no level built on being tired; pain is never part of training.
- Contact: tackles on the feet and for the ball, never into the back; where you can, balance the teams by size and maturity, not by age or level alone; a knock to the head stops play for that player.
- Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a movement that is breaking down; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.

**One thing to think about:** in your new zone, what is your job right now?

**Easier or harder:** easier, longer segments and the zones as starting places only. Harder, see the levels below.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is a small-sided game in which the role changes and the player reads the game again from a new place. The choices are the players': what the new role needs now, where to stand, when to press or cover, when to go.

It is not a trial to find which role suits a child. Two segments say nothing about fit, and the page asks the adult not to read them that way.

## Levels

- **Level 1:** as in the quick start. The zones are only where players start; the change is the one rule.
- **Level 2:** players may go into the next zone to help but come back. Before each segment, about 30 seconds in which the group says what each role's job is; you fill a gap only if nobody does. Every player hears all three across the session.
- **Level 3:** players stay in their zone for the segment. At the change, play does not stop: players have about 5 seconds to reach the new zone while the ball stays live.
- **Level 4:** segments of about 2 minutes, so the changes come often. Before a segment, each role is given one thing to look for ("find the switch pass"); you ask about it afterwards, and nobody has to do it.
- **Level 5:** before each segment, each team has about 30 seconds to agree who takes which role. Every player still plays every zone across the circle; the order is the team's choice.

## For a coach

**Watch for:**

- **The first seconds after a change.** Does the player go straight into the new role, even with a wrong first action, or wait to be told?
- **The look, role by role.** Does the player look for different things as forward, midfielder and defender, or play every zone the same way?
- **Quiet in the new role.** A player who talked as a forward and goes quiet as a defender is often still reading the role. Note it; nobody is asked to call.
- **Ease and unease.** A player who lights up in one role and stiffens in another is telling you where the foundation is thinner. Note it privately; never comment on it in front of others.

**During play:** stay quiet while the ball is moving. "Change" is the rule of the game, said only at the change. Between segments, a question may help ("what was the forward doing that made your job harder?"), never where to stand or what to do.

**One question, after a segment:** "What did you do differently in this zone?"

**Praise** the move into the new role, not the polish: "You went straight into it after the change." "You looked for something different back there."

**Leave for later:** technique in the unfamiliar role, zone discipline at Level 1, and the score.

**If a player drifts back to the zone they like:** at the next change, ask "what is this zone's job?", not a telling-off.

**If a team builds its order around its biggest player (Level 5):** after the segment, ask the group what that does to the other roles. The circle still takes everyone through every zone.

**What not to do:** tell a parent or a player which role suits them after one session, or praise a role over another.

## Why we think it helps

A player who has only played one role reads the game from one place. Here each player meets the defender's problem, the midfielder's and the forward's in one session, and the change makes them read the game again every few minutes. Rotating every role through sixteen is how we keep foundations broad before any specialising, and this game makes the rotation part of the rules rather than a promise. If a group enjoys this and still settles into fixed roles in matches, rotate roles inside your normal games too, at half time or between games.

## Self-assessment

In pairs, at the end:

- "Which role felt easiest today, and which felt strangest? What was different?"
- "When you defended, what was the forward doing that made it harder, and how do you know that now?"
- "What did you notice about the game that you would not have noticed staying in one place?"
