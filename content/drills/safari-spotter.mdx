For a parent and a child of five to eight. Four animal homes sit around a small field. The child chooses which animal to visit, says it, and dribbles through that home's gate; then chooses the next. To choose, the child has to look up and find the homes, and nobody asks for the look. Nothing is counted; the safari ends while the child still wants more.

## Quick start

**You need:** a ball, 8 to 12 markers (cones, shoes, bottles, jumpers or stones), and about 10 m by 10 m of ground (a garden of about 6 m by 6 m works with gates closer together).

**Set it out:**

```
     [parrot]  . .              . .  [lion]
                                               each gate: 2 markers,
                                               about 2.5 m apart
                    (home)

     [elephant] . .             . .  [giraffe]

        <------- about 10 m by 10 m ------->
```

- Four gates, one near each corner, each two markers about 2.5 m apart. A home spot in the middle (a jumper on the ground). All the sizes are suggestions.
- The child names the animals and, with help the first time, puts the markers down. Any animals the child likes.

**One visit:**

1. The child starts at home with the ball, looks round and says which animal they are visiting ("the lion!"), or points.
2. The child dribbles to that home and through its gate.
3. From there, the child chooses the next animal, says it, and goes. Home again whenever they like.
4. Go on for as long as the child wants.

If the child goes through a gate without saying it, nothing is said. If the child forgets which home is which, walk over and show them.

**Load:**

- For 5 to 8: about 10 minutes, ending while they still want more; a drink and a rest whenever they like. Pain is never part of play.
- Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a fall that hurts; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.
- With two children, a bigger field, so nobody bumps.

**One thing to think about:** where is the next animal you want to visit?

**Easier:** fewer homes, closer together, and a walk with the ball instead of a dribble.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is a story game with the ball for a young child. The choice is the child's: which home next. Looking up comes with it, because the homes are only found by looking.

It is not a reaction game and not a test. Nobody calls the animal while the child is moving, nothing is counted, and there is no right or wrong order.

## How it grows

There are no levels. These are ways the safari changes across sessions, as the child is ready.

- **The first safari:** four homes, the child chooses and says each one.
- **A home you can see:** before a visit, you hang a bib or a hat on one gate. The rule of the safari is that the animal with the hat is hungry and wants a visit; the child finds it by looking. Move it while the child is at home, never during a visit.
- **Two in a row:** the child chooses two animals at home ("lion, then parrot!") and visits both in that order.
- **Two children:** each child on their own safari in the same field, choosing their own homes. The other child is now something moving to look out for. Not a race.
- **Swap:** the child chooses the animal, and you (or an older brother or sister) do the visiting. The child watches someone else find the way.
- **New animals:** the child adds a home, moves one, or names a new animal.

## For a parent or coach

**Watch for:**

- The head lifting to find the next home, even briefly, even wobbly, before the child sets off.
- The child saying the animal before arriving, without being asked.
- The child wanting to move a gate, add an animal, or change a rule. Follow it.
- Smiles, and the animals turning into characters.

**During a visit:** stay quiet while the ball is moving. At home, between visits, a question may help ("which animal hasn't had a visit today?"), never which one to go to.

**Praise** the looking and the choosing: "You spotted the parrot from all the way over there!" "You told the elephant you were coming."

**Leave for later:** the dribble itself (wobbly and slow is fine), which foot, the order of the visits.

**If the child charges head down every time:** before the next visit, at home, ask with curiosity: "I wonder where the giraffe is. Can you see it from here?" Once, then let it go.

**If interest drops:** stop, or ask: "Do you want to move the homes, add an animal, or play something else?"

**What not to do:** count visits, time the safari, call the animal while the child is dribbling, or correct the dribble.

## Why we think it helps

A child who has to find the next home with the ball at the feet looks up because the game needs it, not because an adult said so. Here the child chooses the home, so the look and the choice belong to the child, and the story makes it worth coming back to. If the child loves the safari and still dribbles head down in other games, leave it: more safaris, more homes, and a second child in the field do more than a reminder would.

## Self-assessment

Asked lightly, at the end, if the child is in the mood:

- "Which animal was the hardest to find?"
- "Where did you look before you set off?"
- "Which animal should live here next time?"
