For an adult with one to eight children of five to eight, each with a ball. Everyone moves like an animal: a bear's big heavy steps, a crab's sideways scuttle, a frog's little hops, a flamingo standing on one leg. Then the ball comes along, and each animal moves it its own way. A parent, a brother or sister, or a coach can run it.

## Quick start

**You need:** a ball for each child and an open, flat space (a garden, a park, a hall) with room for every child to move without bumping.

**Set it out:**

```
        open space, about 10 m by 10 m for a few children

      (child + ball)       (child + ball)

             (child + ball)        (adult, playing along)
```

- About 10 m by 10 m for two or three children, more for a bigger group. The sizes are suggestions. Nothing to set out; in a hall, keep the children away from the walls.

**How it runs:**

1. **Move like the animal, no ball.** You show an animal and say its name; everyone moves like it. Bear, crab, frog, flamingo.
2. **Add the ball.** Now each child dribbles while moving like the animal: big slow touches for the bear, tiny quick ones for a mouse, sideways for the crab.
3. **A new animal.** When it is time for a new one, you stop, say "new animal!", and show it. Everyone changes.
4. **The children choose.** A child picks the next animal and shows it; everyone follows.

About 5 to 10 minutes in all.

**Load:**

- For 5 to 8: about 5 to 10 minutes, ending while they still want more; a drink and a rest whenever they like. Pain is never part of play.
- Space: enough room that nobody bumps into another child; a child who falls stops for a moment and starts again when ready.
- Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a fall that hurts; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.

**One thing to think about:** how does your animal move the ball?

**Easier:** no ball for longer, and the animals the child already knows.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is movement play with a ball for young children. The choices are the child's: how their bear moves, how their crab touches the ball, which animal comes next, and, later, which new animal to invent.

It is not a technique lesson and not a reaction game. Nobody corrects a touch, nothing is counted, and a new animal is never called while the children are moving.

## How it grows

There are no levels. These are ways the game changes across sessions, as the children are ready.

- **Animals with no ball:** moving like each animal comes first.
- **Animals with the ball:** each animal moves the ball its own way.
- **An animal you can see:** instead of saying the next animal, you start moving like it yourself. The children find the new animal by looking at you and join in.
- **The touch of each animal:** a bear's big heavy touches, a mouse's tiny ones, a flamingo with one foot resting lightly on the ball, the standing foot taking the weight, on grass or a mat, not a hall floor. The children find different touches through the animals.
- **Invent an animal:** a child makes up an animal and its move with the ball; everyone tries it. The game becomes theirs.

## For a parent or coach

**Watch for:**

- Many different ways of moving, and the ball still close enough to keep.
- Happy faces and lots of touches.
- A child inventing an animal, or a new way for an old one to move the ball.
- A shy child copying quietly from the edge: they are still playing.

**While they play:** stay quiet while the animals are moving, or play along. A new animal comes after a stop, or by showing it.

**Praise** the moving and the inventing: "What a heavy bear!" "I've never seen a crab do that with a ball."

**Leave for later:** every point of technique, which foot, how close the ball stays.

**If an animal is too hard** (a deep crab scuttle, say): make it easier or move on to another animal.

**If the ball gets forgotten:** that is fine sometimes. At the next stop, ask: "Can the bear bring the ball?"

**If it gets too serious:** add a silly animal, or let a child lead.

**What not to do:** correct touches, count, compare children, or run it long.

## Why we think it helps

At five to eight, a child who moves in many ways with a ball builds a wide base of movement, and a game the child enjoys is one the child comes back to. Here the animals give the variety and the fun at once, and the invented animals make the game the child's own. If a child loves the animals and still dribbles only one way in other games, leave it: more animals, and the child's own, do more than a correction would.

## One question at the end

Just one, and only if they want to answer:

- "Which animal was the most fun to be with the ball?"
