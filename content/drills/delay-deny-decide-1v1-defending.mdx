For a coach or a parent with two or three players. One attacker tries to dribble through a gate at the end of a narrow channel; the defender has to slow them down, show them one way, and choose the moment to win the ball.

## Quick start

**You need:** 6 cones (4 for the channel, 2 for the gate; 2 more for Level 5) and a ball.

**Set it out:**

```
        TARGET GATE (the defender protects it)
            [G]     [G]     gate about 3 m wide
            |              |
            |   channel    |
            |   about 6 m  |
            |   wide,      |
            |   14 m long  |
            +--------------+
           the attacker starts here with the ball
```

- A channel about 14 m long and 6 m wide, and a gate about 3 m wide at the defender's end. All the sizes are suggestions.
- The defender starts level with the gate and steps up to meet the attacker at a distance of their own choosing.
- A third player, if there is one, waits as the next attacker with a ball.

**One rep:**

1. The attacker goes when ready and tries to dribble through the gate under control.
2. The defender steps up and works three jobs: **delay** (slow the advance by arriving under control, not at full sprint), **deny** (stand side-on and show the attacker to one side), **decide** (go for the ball when the moment comes: a heavy touch, the head down, or the gate in danger).
3. The rep ends on a gate scored (the attacker's point), a ball won under control inside the channel (the defender's point), the ball leaving the channel, or about 10 seconds passing (a draw).
4. Swap roles every 2 reps.

**The first block:** 3 minutes in each role, then 90 seconds rest; two or three blocks each. These numbers are a suggestion: shorten the blocks if the duels get slower.

**Load:**

- Pair players by size and maturity, not by age or level alone; contact is for the ball, from the side or the front, never into the back; the bout ends at about 10 seconds, and the rest above comes between blocks. No level is built on being tired.
- For 9 to 12: about 15 minutes, ending while they still want more; the first block's numbers or fewer.
- Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a movement that is breaking down; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.

**One thing to think about:** arrive slow, stand side-on, and wait for the moment.

**Easier or harder:** easier, a wider channel and an attacker at walking pace. Harder, see the levels below.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is a game problem for two: one attacker, one defender. The choice it trains is the defender's: when to go for the ball, read from the attacker's body and touch, and which side to show.

It does not train defending with a teammate covering, or defending in a back line; those need more players.

## Levels

- **Level 1:** as in the quick start.
- **Level 2:** a ball won after showing the attacker to one side and keeping them there scores 2. The defender chooses the side from what they see (the attacker's stronger foot, the line of the channel), and the coach asks after the rep which side and why.
- **Level 3:** the channel narrows to about 5 m. With less room the attacker relies more on a body feint, so reading the body matters more.
- **Level 4:** the attacker starts 2 m ahead of the defender, so the defender begins behind and must recover an angle before they can delay. The ball stays the aim once the defender gets there.
- **Level 5:** a second gate about 3 m wide beside the first, with a cone between them (2 more cones). The attacker chooses which to attack; the defender has to hold a position that gives away neither too early, reading the attacker's commitment before moving across.

## For a parent or coach

**Watch for:**

- **The approach.** Does the defender arrive under control and drop into a low, balanced stance, or arrive at full sprint and get passed?
- **The body angle.** Side-on and showing one way, or square-on and easy to go round on either side?
- **The moment.** Does the defender go for the ball when the touch is heavy or the head drops, or lunge at an attacker who has the ball close?

**One question, between reps:** "What did the attacker's body tell you before they went?"

**During a rep:** stay quiet while the ball is moving. When a player is stuck, a question or a short cue may help ("what are the hips doing?"), never when to tackle.

**Praise** the read as well as the tackle: "You waited, and went when the touch got heavy." A defender who kept the attacker from the gate and was beaten late has still done most of the work.

**Leave for later:** the technique of the tackle itself. First the choice of when; the tackle sharpens once the timing is read.

**When to simplify:** if the defender is beaten on almost every rep, lengthen the channel, or slow the attacker to a jog.

## Why we think it helps

A young defender who is judged only by whether the ball was won tends to lunge at the first touch, get beaten, and learn little from it. This drill makes the defender's choice visible: the narrow channel takes away the attacker's room to run round the problem, so slowing them, showing them one way and waiting for the moment become the way to win the ball. A beaten rep is worth one question about what the attacker did. If a defender reads well here and still dives in during games, move the same picture into a 2v2 where a teammate can cover.

## Self-assessment

- "Which of the three jobs (delay, deny, decide) felt strongest today? Which needs work?"
- "When you got beaten, what did the attacker do? Could you see it coming next time?"
- "How would this feel with a real goal and a keeper behind you?"
