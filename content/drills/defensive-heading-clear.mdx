## Introduction

Defensive heading is a real football skill, and like every physical skill it is sequenced and loaded with care. Your football association's rules decide when heading starts, and nothing on this page overrides them. The page is written for players of thirteen and over. The work is technique-first, at low volume, and only once the player has the neck and trunk readiness to absorb the contact safely (mobility before strength, strength before speed; the sequence is not skippable, and heading sits on a base of trunk and neck stability that has to exist first). This is a skill drill, not a heading-volume session: few reps, clean technique, a ball the player meets cleanly, and an immediate stop if a player reports any head discomfort.

Within those guardrails, the skill is clear. Defensive heading is about attacking the ball: meeting it on the forehead, with the eyes open and the neck firm, and sending it up and away from danger, wide of goal. Direction matters more than distance; distance follows when the technique is there (specificity wins; the defensive header is trained as the exact game action, not an abstract). It takes courage and composure to attack a dropping ball with a committed jump rather than flinch away from it, and that bravery is a trainable Affective skill, built through graded, successful reps (mental resilience is trainable; the composure to attack the ball is grown, not innate). Quality reps, kept few and clean, are how the technique installs, and the drill overdoes the match's demand only in precision, never in volume or force (the training ball is delivered to be headable; the match's awkward ball feels manageable after clean technique is grooved).

## Setup

```
        [SERVER tosses a soft, headable ball up and toward the player]
                 ↑  ⚽
        •──────────────•          TARGET: head the ball up and away,
        |  jump + head  |         back past the server or to a side
        •──────────────•
   (early reps: served gently from 3–4m; later: a longer flight, same gentle pace)
```

- **Player** in a marked spot; **server** tosses a **soft or lightweight ball** for the early reps (a normal ball only once technique is sound).
- **Target:** send the ball up and away, back over the server or to a side of a line.
- Keep rep counts low (a handful per set, a few sets) with full rest; this is technique work, not conditioning.

## Description

**Technique taught first (no jump, gentle ball):**

1. **Eyes open, mouth closed, neck firm.** The forehead (the hard, flat part above the brow) is the contact surface, never the top of the head.
2. **Attack the ball.** Meet it; do not let it hit you. The body moves into the ball, not away.
3. **Up and away.** Contact the lower half of the ball to send it up; aim it back where it came from or to a side, never down into danger. How far it goes comes later.

**One rep:**

1. The server tosses a soft, headable ball up toward the player.
2. The player reads the flight, times the movement, and attacks the ball, jumping to meet it at the top once the standing technique is sound (the jump is added only after the contact is safe and clean).
3. Contact on the forehead, neck firm, eyes open; the ball goes up and away from goal.
4. Keep volume low: a few reps, full rest, technique reviewed between sets.

The measure is **clean contact and direction away from danger**, at low volume. Distance follows when it comes. It is never how many headers, and never headers to exhaustion.

## Progressions

- **Level 1 (baseline):** standing, soft ball, gentle service from 3–4m; groove forehead contact and the up-and-away direction with no jump.
- **Level 2 (add the jump):** once standing contact is sound, add a two-footed jump to meet the ball at its height: attack it at the top.
- **Level 3 (normal ball, longer flight):** a normal ball, still served at a pace the player meets cleanly; the flight is a little longer, so the player reads more of it. The force does not rise.
- **Level 4 (under a challenge):** a passive partner stands close (presence, no contact); the player must attack the ball and clear it despite someone in the space; this is the first bravery layer (the composure to commit to the header with a body near).
- **Level 5 (game-like clearance):** a few game-like attempts, not sets: a ball served from the side as a cross would come, at a pace the player meets cleanly, with other players nearby and no contact in the space. The player attacks it from a short run-up and clears it off either side. No head-to-head, elbow or undercutting risk: winning the ball never justifies it.

## Heading and load

**Heading and load.** Your football association's rules on heading by age come first, and nothing on this page overrides them. We give no number of headers: keep heading practice short, never repetitive, never high-force and never without an adult watching closely. A defensive clearance asks for force, so serve the ball at a pace the player can meet cleanly, not as hard as possible. Watch four things: how many headers, how hard the ball arrives, which ball you use, and how much rest comes between, and count all the heading the player does across club, school and their own practice, not only this session. With younger players, use a lighter ball where the rules allow it. If a player may have a concussion, stop at once: no more play that day, and a medical check before they return.

## Coach guidance

**Look for:**

- **Forehead contact, eyes open.** The hard forehead, never the top of the head; eyes open through contact. This is the safety-and-quality core.
- **Attacking the ball.** Does the player move into the ball with a committed jump, or wait and flinch? Attacking it is both safer (controlled contact) and better (more power), and it takes composure.
- **Direction.** Up and away, wide of goal. A header that drops down or sideways into danger is a technique miss. Distance is the last thing to look at.

**Cues:** *"Eyes open, watch it onto your forehead."* · *"Attack it: go and meet it, don't wait."* · *"Up and away, back where it came from."* · *"Firm neck, contact on your forehead."*

**Praise:** the brave, clean technique. *"You attacked that one: forehead, eyes open, up and away from goal. That's a defender's header."* (Name the courage as the trained skill.)

**Don't fix yet / safety first:** never push heading volume to build "toughness"; keep reps low and clean. Stop immediately if a player reports any head discomfort. If a player is hesitant in a way that suggests fear or fatigue, use a softer ball and fewer reps rather than pressing on. Power comes from technique and timing, not from more reps.

## Watch points

- **The player closes their eyes** at contact. *"Eyes open: you have to see it onto your forehead."* Closed eyes mean mistimed, unsafe contact.
- **The ball hits the top of the head** rather than the forehead. *"Get under it and use your forehead, the flat part above your eyes."*
- **The player waits for the ball and flinches.** *"Attack it. Go and meet it. Waiting is when it goes wrong."*
- **The clearance drops down into danger.** *"Get under the ball and send it up and away."*
- **Any sign of head discomfort or rising rep counts.** Stop. Heading is technique work at low volume, never a session you push through.

## Closing reflection

- *"Did you attack the ball or wait for it? What was the difference in the clearance?"*
- *"Where did your best headers go, and were any heading down into danger?"*
- *"How did it feel to commit to the jump? What helps you attack the ball?"*
