For a coach with six to ten players aged thirteen and over. Two small teams play to gates at each end. A team that wins the ball and gets through a gate quickly scores double, so on every turnover the player on the ball has to decide: is the break on, or do we keep it?

## Quick start

**You need:** 12 markers (8 for the gates, 4 for the corners), bibs in two colours, several balls, and an area about 28 m by 22 m.

**Set it out:**

```
   [gate]        [gate]
   +-------------------+
   |                   |
   |   3v3 or 4v4      |
   |   about 28 m x    |
   |   22 m            |
   +-------------------+
   [gate]        [gate]
```

- Two gates at each end, each two markers about 2 m apart. Each team attacks the two gates at the far end and defends its own two.
- Two teams of 3 or 4. With 7 or 9 players, one rests and swaps in after each game; with 10, two rest.
- A gate is scored by dribbling through it, or passing to a teammate who controls the ball through it.

**How it runs:**

1. The teams play. When a team wins the ball, the coach counts six seconds silently and says nothing during play. The player on the ball reads from the defenders getting back whether the break is still on.
2. A gate scored inside six seconds scores 2. A gate scored later, from a settled attack, scores 1. The coach gives the score at the gate ("two" or "one").
3. The player who wins the ball decides: go forward now, or keep the ball and build. Keeping it when the break is not on is a good choice, not a lost point.
4. Teammates of the player who won it run forward into space, away from the ball, so the forward pass is there.
5. When the ball goes out or a gate is scored, the coach plays a new ball in at once, to either team.

**The first block:** games of 2 minutes, then 2 minutes rest; four to six games in all. These numbers are a suggestion: shorten the games if the runs get slow.

**Load:**

- The breaks are short sprints over and over. Rest between games is real rest. Stop the session when the runs get slower and sloppier, and never use extra running as a punishment.
- Tackles are for the ball, from the side or the front, never into the back. Split teams by size and maturity, not by age or level alone.
- Rest between games is kept for every band; nobody plays on tired to make it harder.
- Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a movement that is breaking down; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.

**One thing to think about:** when you win the ball, look forward first.

**Easier or harder:** easier, see Level 1. Harder, see the levels below.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is a game problem for a small team. The choice it trains is the ball-winner's: break now or keep it, read from the runs and the space in front.

It does not train winning the ball back after losing it. [The Five Seconds](/training/counter-press-five-seconds) trains that, and the two run well one after the other.

## Levels

- **Level 1:** eight seconds for the double. Teams learn to look forward on winning the ball.
- **Level 2:** six seconds, as in the quick start.
- **Level 3:** the double counts only if a teammate was already running forward before the pass or the dribble through the gate.
- **Level 4:** keeping it also scores: a team that wins the ball, sees the break is not on and keeps it for five passes scores 1.
- **Level 5:** when the coach plays a new ball in, it sometimes goes to a team with one extra player for that phase (a resting player joins for one attack). The extra player is there only briefly, so the break has to be quick. With no one resting, the coach joins the team for one attack, or skip Level 5.

## For a parent or coach

**Watch for:**

- **The first look.** When a player wins the ball, do they look forward or sideways first?
- **The runs.** Do teammates run forward into space the moment the ball is won, or stand and ask for it to their feet?
- **The choice.** Is the forward ball a real break, or a hopeful kick into nobody? And when it is not on, do they keep it?

**One question, between games:** "When you won it, what did you see first, and how did you know whether to go or keep it?"

**During play:** nothing but the score at the gate. Questions and cues wait for the break.

**Praise** both good choices: "You won it, saw the runner and went in one touch." "The break was not on, so you kept it. Good read."

**Leave for later:** the exact weight of the forward pass. First the look and the choice; the pass sharpens after.

**When to simplify:** if almost every turnover ends with a hopeful kick, go back to Level 1 and add Level 4's scoring early.

## Why we think it helps

Just after a team loses the ball it is often spread out and not yet back in place. A team that sees this and goes forward at once can turn a tackle into a chance. Going forward every time is not the aim: the game scores the read, and keeping the ball is a good answer when the break is not on. If players read the break well here and not in matches, the next step is a bigger game with the same scoring, not more of this one.

## Self-assessment

- "When you won the ball, what did you look at first?"
- "When was the break on, and when should you have kept it? How could you tell?"
- "What did the quickest breaks have in common?"
