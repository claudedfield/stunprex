## Quick start

**You need:** a quiet place where nobody will disturb you, and a notebook or a notes app. No ball. You do this alone. It takes about 6 minutes.

**The four steps:**

1. **Pick one moment (1 minute).** Choose one moment from your game that you already practise with the ball. Write it in one sentence that says where you are, who is near you and what you want to do. "Playing well" is too wide. "I receive with my back to goal, a defender tight behind me, and I want to turn" is the right size.
2. **Settle (1 minute).** Sit down. Close your eyes, or rest them on one spot. Take five slow breaths.
3. **Run it four times (about 2 minutes).** See the moment through your own eyes, at the speed of a game. Each run lasts only a few seconds. Take a slow breath and set the picture again before the next one.
   - First, three runs that go well: what you see and feel before the ball arrives, what you decide, what you do, and what happens next.
   - Then one run that goes wrong: the touch gets away, or the defender wins the ball. See what you do in the next few seconds: you breathe out, look up, and do the next thing well.
4. **Write two lines (2 minutes).** How clear was the picture? What did you decide in it, and why?

**An example.** Moment: "I receive with my back to goal, a defender tight behind me, and I want to turn." A run: I look over my shoulder as the pass is played and see the defender on my left. I decide to go right. I take the ball with my right foot, away from them, turn out, and I am facing goal. The run that goes wrong: my touch is heavy and the defender takes the ball. I breathe out, look up and chase back. Two lines: "Clear until the turn, then blurry. I turned right because the defender was on my left."

**When it goes wrong:**

- Your mind wanders: start that run again. It is no fault.
- You see yourself from outside, as on a screen: start the run again from where you stand, looking at the ball.
- You cannot see the moment clearly: make it shorter, down to the look and the first touch, or watch a clip of it first.
- Every run turns into the one that goes wrong: stop for today, and pick a simpler moment next time.

**One thing to think about:** the decision. Do not skip past it to the good ending.

**Easier or harder:** easier is a shorter moment. Harder is a defender who does something different in each run.

**How much:** one moment and four runs is enough for one sitting. Stop if it leaves you more tense than when you started. If that happens more than once, leave this practice out and keep to the work with the ball.

## What this is, and what it is not

This is a supporting practice for a player alone, with no ball. It has no layout and nobody to play against. The moment and the decision in it are yours. The four steps are the fixed part.

It goes beside the work with the ball. It does not replace any of it. Do not use it to learn a skill you do not have yet. Rehearse only what you already practise with the ball, in a drill from [the drill library](/training) or with your coach. If you cannot do the turn on the pitch yet, practise the turn there first.

Nothing in it tells you whether the decision you pictured was the right one. You find that out on the pitch. What you can check here is whether the picture was clear and whether you made a decision in it at all.

## Doing it over weeks

The numbers here and in the quick start are our suggestions to start from. None is a tested dose.

- Keep it short. We suggest a few minutes on several days of the week rather than one long sitting.
- Stay with one moment for a week or two before you choose another.
- Before you start, read your last two lines.
- Once a week, add a third line: how did that moment go in training or in a match this week?
- If the third line says the moment went better, stay with it a little longer or move to the next one. If it says nothing has changed after two or three weeks, change something: a smaller moment, a clip to watch first, or more work on it with the ball.

## What good looks like

- The moment is one sentence long and has a decision in it.
- You see it from inside, through your own eyes. You are not watching yourself from the stand.
- In each run you can say what you saw before you decided.
- The run that goes wrong ends with you doing the next thing, not with the mistake.
- The two lines say what happened in the picture. They do not say what kind of player you are.

## Ways to change it

Stay with the base version until the picture is clear in most runs. Then try one of these. You do not have to take them in order.

- **A different picture each run (harder).** Change what the defender does: tight on your left, tight on your right, standing off. Decide from what you see in each run. Go back to one picture if the runs blur into each other.
- **With a clip (easier).** Watch a short clip of the moment, your own or somebody else's, two or three times before step 2. Leave the clip out again once you can see the moment without it.
- **Before the drill (different).** Do the four runs just before you practise the same moment with the ball. Write your two lines after the drill and say whether the pitch looked like the picture. If it makes the start of the drill feel rushed, go back to doing the runs at a quiet time.
- **After a match (different).** Pick one real moment from the match that you would play differently. Run it once as it happened and three times with the decision you would make now. Do this only when you can think about the match calmly. If going over it leaves you more wound up, leave it and go back to the base version.
- **The reset alone (different).** If the run that goes wrong is the hard part for you, [The Reset Ritual](/training/reset-ritual-after-mistake) practises the same few seconds with a ball and a wall.

## For a coach

This practice belongs to the player, and you cannot see it. Your part is to ask.

**Questions to ask:**

- "Which moment did you pick, and do you practise it with the ball?"
- "What did you see before you decided?"
- "How did that moment go this week?"

**Leave alone:** the player's choice of moment, and how they describe the picture.

**Step in, or stop it, when:**

- the moment is one the player does not practise on the pitch: put it into their work with the ball first;
- the rehearsal starts to take the place of sessions with the ball: say so plainly;
- the player reports that it makes them more anxious about the moment: leave it out, and talk with the player about how the moment goes on the pitch.

## Why we think it helps

This is our reasoning, and we have not tested it. A moment in a match is over in a second or two and may come only a few times in a game. Running it through in your head lets you meet it more often, and lets you practise the look and the decision without anything else going on. Trust in the moment still has to come from what you have done with the ball. The rehearsal is an addition to that, not a replacement.

**Where it should show later:** in a match, when the moment comes, you can say afterwards what you saw and what you decided. After a mistake in it, your next action follows without a long pause.

**What would count against it:** after several weeks of rehearsing, the moment in your matches is unchanged and you still cannot say what you saw. Or the minutes spent rehearsing have come out of your time with the ball.
