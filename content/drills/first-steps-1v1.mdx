For a coach of five to eight year olds. One child dribbles at a goal; another stands in the way, moves side to side with arms out, and never tackles. The child with the ball looks, picks the side that seems more open and goes. Nobody counts goals and nobody wins, so the moment of facing a person stays interesting rather than frightening.

## Quick start

**You need:** 4 cones for the corners, 2 cones for a goal, 1 ball, and about 10 m by 8 m of flat ground.

**Set it out:**

```
      G  . . . . . . .  G      goal about 4 m wide
              D                defender about 2 m in front of the goal
      .                 .
      .                 .      area about 10 m long, 8 m wide
      .                 .
              A                attacker with the ball, at the far end
```

- An area about 10 m long and 8 m wide. At one end, a goal about 4 m wide (wide on purpose, so a choice that works ends in a goal). All the sizes are suggestions.
- The defender (D) stands about 2 m in front of the goal, facing the attacker (A). The adult stands outside the area, at the side.

**One rep:**

1. A starts at the far end with the ball, and goes when ready.
2. A dribbles at D and tries to go around D and through the goal.
3. D moves side to side with arms out to close the way, feet staying roughly in place until A is close. D never tackles and never touches A.
4. The rep ends when the ball goes through the goal, goes out of the area, or the rep stalls for about 12 seconds; then the adult says "again". Nothing is counted, and nobody says which side would have worked.
5. Back to the start. With three children, the third fetches balls and swaps in: change roles every 4 reps. With two, swap after each block.

**Load:**

- For 5 to 8: blocks of about 2 minutes, then about 1 minute of easy play with the ball; three blocks at most, about 8 to 10 minutes in all, ending while they still want more. A drink and a rest whenever they like. Pain is never part of play.
- Contact: none at Levels 1 to 3. From Level 4, see the level's line.
- Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a fall that hurts; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.

**One thing to think about:** which side looks more open, before you get close.

**Easier or harder:** easier, D stands still with arms out. Harder, see the levels below.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is a first 1v1 game for young children. The choice is the child's: which side to go, read from where the defender stands and leans.

It is not a contest. Nobody counts goals, nobody is the winner, and the adult does not tell the child which side to take or when to look.

## Levels

- **Level 1:** as in the quick start. D moves side to side only.
- **Level 2:** D starts about 3 m in front of the goal and may step towards A as A comes. Still no tackle. The open side changes sooner, so A chooses earlier.
- **Level 3:** the goal narrows to about 3 m.
- **Level 4:** D may try once per rep to tap the ball away with one foot, staying where they stand. No tackle, no touching A. A learns to keep the ball close when near D.
- **Level 5:** D defends for real, on the feet, for the ball only: no sliding, no pushing, no holding, never into the back. Pair children by size, not by age alone; a knock to the head stops play for that child. This is the bridge into the full 1v1.

## For a parent or coach

**Watch for:**

- **The look.** Does the child look at the goal and at D before going, or only at the ball? A quick glance counts. Nobody asks for it; you are watching whether it comes.
- **A side chosen.** Does the child commit to one side? Any committed direction is what you are looking for, whether or not it ends in a goal. A child who slows in the middle and stops is not choosing yet.
- **After a rep that did not work.** Does the child come back keen, or drop their head?
- **D.** If D stays so still that A walks through, ask D before the next rep: "Can you make them choose?"

**During a rep:** stay quiet while the ball is moving. Before a rep, a question may help ("which side looks more open?"), never which side to take.

**One question, after a rep that did not work:** "What happened? What would you try next?" Wait for any answer and move on.

**Praise** the look and the choice, not the goal: "You looked before you went." "You picked a side and went for it." "You came back for the next one." After a goal, keep it short: "Through the gap. Nice."

**Leave for later:** which foot, and the order of the look. Any foot and any touch is fine. Now and then, between reps, you might ask "what if you went the other way this time?", never as a correction.

**If the child freezes near D:** before the next rep, make it easier (D stands still), and say something warm: "You were close. Have another go."

**What not to do:** count goals, name a winner, compare one child with another, or call a side.

## Why we think it helps

A child facing a person with the ball has to look, choose and go, and a person moves in a way a cone does not. Here the defender cannot tackle, the goal is wide and nothing is scored, so the choice comes often and a miss costs nothing. We think the look grows when it is useful to the child, not when it is asked for: the open side is only there for the child who sees it. If a child enjoys this and still freezes in games, play it longer at an easier level before adding the tap or the tackle.

## Self-assessment

Asked lightly, after a block, if the child is in the mood:

- "Which side did you go most? Why that one?"
- "What did the defender do that made you change your mind?"
- "Was there a time you went the other way?"
