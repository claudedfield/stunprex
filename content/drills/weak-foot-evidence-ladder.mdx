## Quick start

**You need:** a ball, a solid wall at least 3 m wide with no windows near it, 4 markers, a phone timer and a notebook or a notes app. Flat ground about 10 m deep in front of the wall and about 5 m wide. You do this alone. The first block takes 3 minutes.

**Which foot:** the one you use less. If you are right-footed, that is your left.

**Set it out:**

```
   ██████████████████   wall
            ↑
           5 m
            ↓
        •       •       front edge of the box
          (you)         the box: 3 m × 3 m
        •       •       back edge, 8 m from the wall
```

Mark a box of 3 m × 3 m with the four markers. Its front edge is 5 m from the wall.

**The first block, wall passes (3 minutes):**

1. Stand in the box with the ball and start the timer.
2. Pass against the wall with the weaker foot.
3. Stop the ball as it comes back, with the weaker foot.
4. If your first touch kept the ball inside the box, that is a clean one. Say the running number out loud ("one", "two").
5. Pass again. Every pass is played from inside the box. If the ball has left it, bring it back in first. Keep going until the timer rings, then write the number down.

**What does not count:** a pass or a touch with the other foot, or a ball that your first touch sends out of the box. Nothing is lost. Carry on with the next pass.

**When it goes wrong:**

- The ball misses the wall or runs away: fetch it and carry on. The timer keeps running.
- The timer rings while the ball is on its way back: that one does not count.
- You lose count: write your best guess with a question mark beside it.

**How hard:** a steady pace, with firm passes, not as hard as you can. Stop if something hurts, or if you are tired and the touches are getting worse.

**One thing to think about:** a first touch that leaves the ball ready for the next pass. Count only the clean ones.

**Easier or harder:** easier is a box of 4 m × 4 m, harder a box of 2 m × 2 m. The front edge stays 5 m from the wall.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is a supporting drill for practising on your own. It gives your weaker foot a lot of passes, first touches and strikes in a short time, and it gives you a number for each block that you can read back later.

There is no real choice in it. A wall sends the ball back the way it was hit, so there is no passer to read, and there is no opponent. It works on how the foot meets the ball. It does not work on when to use that foot in a game. [Weak-Foot Dribbling Gauntlet](/training/weak-foot-dribbling-gauntlet) is the same idea for dribbling. [First Rondo](/training/3v1-rondo-foundation), for four players, is a drill where you do not get to choose which side the ball arrives on.

## A full session

About 15 minutes, all with the weaker foot. We suggest two or three sessions a week with a day between them. That is a suggestion, not a tested dose.

- **Warm the foot (2 minutes).** In the box: roll the ball under the sole and move it between the inside and the outside of the foot. Nothing is counted.
- **Block 1, wall passes (3 minutes),** as in the quick start. Then 1 minute rest, and write the number down.
- **Block 2, stop and strike (3 minutes).** Stand on the back edge of the box. Strike the ball against the wall with the laces, firmly, not as hard as you can. Stop the rebound with the weaker foot. A clean one is a rebound that your first touch keeps inside the box. Take the ball back to the back edge with the weaker foot before each strike. Those touches are not counted. Then 1 minute rest, and write the number down.
- **Block 3, close touches (3 minutes).** Stay inside the box and keep the ball moving with the weaker foot only: rolls, drags, pull-backs. Here you count the other way round, so a lower number is the better one. Every time the ball leaves the box or the other foot touches it, say the running number out loud. Write that number down.
- **One line.** Under the three numbers, write one sentence about how it went. For example: "Passes cleaner than last time. Strikes still going wide."
- **Compare** the three numbers with your last entry, and choose one block to give your best attention next time.

An entry can look like this: "12 Oct. Passes 31. Strikes 14. Close touches, out 6 times. Left foot fine on the pass, strikes still going wide."

**How to read the numbers:**

- Compare them only with your own earlier entries. They say nothing about anybody else.
- If a number is better than last time, look at your one line and see what you did differently.
- If a number is worse than last time, write it down just the same. A tired day, a different wall or a harder ball can all do that, and the entry is only useful if it is true.
- Use the same wall and the same box when you can, so the numbers can be compared. If something was different, write it in the entry.

## What good looks like

- The pass comes off the wall along the ground and back to the box.
- The first touch stops the ball where the next pass can be played from.
- In block 2 the strike is firm and you are balanced after it.
- In block 3 the ball stays within a step of you.
- The count has only clean ones in it, and the bad days are in the notebook too.

## Ways to change it

Stay with the base version for four or five sessions, so that you have entries to compare. Then try one of these. You do not have to take them in order. Write in the entry which version you did, because the numbers of one version cannot be compared with another.

- **A stricter clean one (harder).** A pass or strike counts only if your first touch stops the ball within one step of you. Go back to the box rule if your count drops to almost nothing.
- **A target on the wall (harder).** Mark a target about 1 m wide on the wall with chalk or tape, or stand two more markers against it. A pass or strike counts only if it hits the target and the first touch is clean. Go back to the whole wall if you are fetching the ball more than playing it.
- **Two touches (harder).** In block 1, the first touch stops the ball and the second passes it. A third touch means that one does not count. Go back to free touches if the passes turn wild.
- **Another surface (different, not harder).** Do one session on a hard court, or on rough grass. Write the surface in the entry and compare that session only with others on the same surface.
- **The other foot (a check).** Every few weeks, do block 1 once more with your stronger foot and write that number beside the weaker foot's. The gap between the two is what this drill is working on.

## For a parent or coach

The drill and the notebook belong to the player. Your part is small.

**Watch for:**

- a count with messy touches in it;
- the stronger foot stepping in and the pass being counted anyway;
- no entry on a bad day;
- a player who compares the number with a friend's.

**Questions to ask:**

- "Which of the three numbers would you most like to change?"
- "What did you write in your one line today?"
- "What does your last entry say?"

**If you praise,** praise an honest entry or a session done on a day the player did not feel like it. Do not praise a good number by itself.

**Leave alone for now:** how the technique looks, and how big the numbers are. Do not set targets for the player.

**Make it easier, or stop, when:**

- the counting gets in the way of the playing: count block 1 only, and do the other two without numbers;
- the numbers upset the player: put the notebook away for a session and do the three blocks without it;
- the player has had enough: finish for today;
- something hurts: stop.

## Why we think it helps

This is our reasoning, and we have not tested it. A weaker foot often gets few touches in a game, because the player can use the other one. Here it gets all of them. We also think that a record you can read back is a better reason to trust that foot than somebody telling you it is good. The notebook cannot make the foot better. It can show you what has changed and what has not.

**Where it should show later:** in a small game or a rondo, the ball comes to your weaker side and you pass or control it with that foot, where before you would have turned onto the other one.

**What would count against it:** the numbers in the notebook get better over the weeks, and in games you still turn onto your stronger foot every time.
