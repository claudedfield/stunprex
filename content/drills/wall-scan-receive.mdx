## Quick start

**You need:** a ball, a wall, two markers and a phone timer or a watch. Shoes or bottles will do as markers; with nothing to hand, use a crack in the ground or the edge of a door. You need flat ground about 6 m out from the wall and 4 m wide. Indoors, use a soft ball. You do this alone.

**Set it out:** stand 4 m from the wall. Put one marker 1.5 m to your left and the other 1.5 m to your right, each one step behind you, so that you have to turn your head to see it.

```
   ███████████ WALL ███████████
              ↑
             4 m
              ↓
           [you]
   marker   1.5 m   1.5 m   marker
   (one step behind you, on each side)
```

**One repetition:**

1. Pass firmly along the ground into the wall.
2. While the ball is coming back, turn your head and look at the right marker, then the left one.
3. Take your first touch towards one of the markers, so the ball stops within a step of it. The rule for today: not the same marker twice in a row.
4. Bring the ball back to the middle and pass again.

**When it goes wrong:** if the touch runs away from you, or you skipped the look, that repetition does not count. If the ball misses the wall or gets past you, walk after it and start again from the middle. There is no hurry.

**The first block:** 4 minutes passing and receiving with your right foot, 30 seconds rest, then 4 minutes with your left. Keep a steady pace, easy enough that you could still talk. Stop early if something hurts, or if you are tired and your touch is getting worse.

**One thing to think about:** look while the ball is travelling, not after it has arrived.

**Easier or harder:** if the ball keeps escaping, step back to 5 m, move the markers back with you and pass a little softer. If almost every repetition is clean, step in to 3 m and bring the markers in too.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is a supporting drill for practising on your own. It works on two things. One is the receiving touch, with both feet. The other is taking your eyes off the ball while it travels and still controlling it when it arrives.

It does not teach you to read an opponent or a teammate, and there is no decision in it. The markers do not move and the rule tells you which one comes next, so when you look over your shoulder there is nothing new to find. What you are practising is the movement of the look and its timing. You learn what the look is for when you play with other people, because then somebody behind you is really moving. [Turn Out of Trouble](/training/receive-turn-back-pressure) is a drill for that, for three or four players from about 13.

You still choose three things yourself: how hard you pass, which part of the foot you use and how far you turn your head.

## How a session runs

- If you like, count the clean repetitions in each block: the look was finished before the ball arrived, and the touch ended within a step of the marker. You are the judge.
- Afterwards, take two minutes to think back. Which foot was harder? When did you skip the look?
- Compare your counts only with your own earlier sessions. They say nothing about anybody else.
- Short and often is better than long and rare. Ten minutes is a full session.

## What good looks like

- Your head turns while the ball is on its way back. If you look after it has arrived, the look came too late for this drill.
- The pass into the wall stays firm. A soft pass gives you more time, and then the drill is not asking much of you.
- The first touch moves the ball out of your feet and towards the marker. It does not stop dead under you.
- Both feet get the same number of minutes, including the foot you like less.
- If your two counts are the same in every session, check how you are counting.

## Ways to change it

Stay with the base version until most repetitions in both blocks are clean. Then try one of these. They are not a ladder; pick the one that suits the day.

- **Closer (harder).** Stand 3 m from the wall and bring the markers in with you. The ball comes back sooner, so the look has less time. Go back to 4 m if fewer than half your repetitions are clean.
- **Odd and even (harder, a thinking task).** Count your repetitions out loud. Odd numbers go to the left marker, even numbers to the right. Now you have to keep the count going while your feet work. Go back to the base rule if you lose the count more often than you keep it.
- **Another wall, another ball (different, not harder).** A rougher wall, a different floor or another ball changes how the ball comes back. Use this now and then. The first session at a new wall will be untidy, and that is fine.
- **One touch (harder).** In the second half of each block, play the returning ball straight back to the wall with one touch, and still look at both markers while it travels. A clean repetition here: the look was finished before the contact, and the ball went back to the wall along the ground. Go back to two touches if the ball keeps leaving the ground or missing the wall.
- **With a helper (a check on the look).** Somebody stands about 3 m behind you, in line with the ball. For this one you need about 8 m of ground out from the wall. Drop the rule about alternating. Each time the ball is on its way back, the helper takes one big step to the left or to the right, in no fixed order, and says nothing. Look, find them, and take your first touch towards the marker on the other side. Then the helper steps back to the middle. Between repetitions they can tell you if you went towards them. Now the look has something to find. If you are guessing more than finding, go back to the base version for a while.

## Coaching yourself

For a parent or a coach who shows the drill once and then steps back, and for the player.

**One thing to say to yourself:** "Firm pass."

**One question for afterwards:** "When did I skip the look today, and what was happening?"

**Leave it alone for now:** an untidy touch on the first day at a new wall or with a new rule. Give it a session before you judge it.

**Make it easier, or stop, when:**

- the rule confuses you: go back to "not the same marker twice";
- the same miss keeps coming and you are not learning from it: step back a metre, move the markers back with you and slow the pass;
- you are bored: change the rule, or finish for today;
- something hurts: stop.

## Why we think it helps

This is our reasoning, and we have not tested it. In a game, one of the moments to look is while the ball is on its way to you. Looking away from a moving ball and still controlling it is a skill of its own, and a wall lets you practise it as often as you want, with nobody watching.

**Where it should show later:** a teammate passes to you in a small game or a rondo, with somebody behind you. Your head turns while the ball is travelling, and your first touch still goes where you meant it to go.

**What would count against it:** you look over your shoulder at the wall and nowhere else. Or in games you still watch the ball all the way onto your foot.
