For a coach with two to six players. In pairs, one player keeps the ball in a small square with the body between the ball and the challenger, for a few seconds at a time; the other tries fairly to win it. The strength is built in the contest itself, in short sets with real rest.

## Quick start

**You need:** a ball and 4 cones for each pair (4 more cones per pair from Level 3).

**Set it out:**

```
        +-----------+
        |           |
        |   A  D    |    a square about 5 m by 5 m per pair
        |   ball    |
        |           |
        +-----------+
   pairs about 5 m apart, so nobody backs into another square
```

- One square about 5 m by 5 m per pair, the squares about 5 m apart. All the sizes are suggestions.
- With an odd number, the spare player keeps time for one pair and swaps in every set.

**One bout:**

1. A starts in the middle of the square with the ball at the feet. D starts an arm's length away, beside A and in A's view.
2. D goes when ready and challenges for the ball. A keeps it inside the square: low, the body between D and the ball, the ball on the foot further from D.
3. The bout ends on about 6 seconds with the ball kept (A's point), the ball won or poked out of the square (D's point), or a foul by either player (no point, the bout starts again).
4. Swap roles every bout. The other player of a group of three keeps time and says "time" when the bout is over, never during it. With no third player, the adult says "time"; with several pairs, the bouts start together and one "time" ends them all.

**The first set:** 6 bouts each, about 6 seconds each with about 20 seconds between bouts, then 2 to 3 minutes easy (passing, walking, a drink); two or three sets in all. These numbers are a suggestion: use fewer if the bodies get higher and the contests get scrappier.

**Load:**

- Contact: pair players by size and maturity, not by age or level alone. D challenges for the ball from the side, shoulder to shoulder; a push in the back, a hold, a pull, or a kick at the legs ends the bout. A's arm stays low and close: it feels where D is, never holds or pushes. No one is taken to the ground; a player who falls stays down and the bout stops.
- Strength: every bout is a full effort, so the rest above is part of the drill. Add bouts, time or pressure only when the player still holds a low, balanced position in the bouts already set; keep the numbers where they are when the position breaks down.
- Around a growth spurt, watch the player's balance and position more closely and lower the numbers if they slip; pain near a joint means stop, not push on.
- One adult can see every square. Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a movement that is breaking down; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.

**One thing to think about:** low and wide before the challenge arrives, not after.

**Easier or harder:** easier, D challenges at half effort and the bout lasts about 4 seconds. Harder, see the levels below.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is a supporting drill for the duel, with a choice in it. The choice is A's: how to stand, and from Level 3 when to turn and where to play out, read from where D comes.

It is not resistance training with weights, and it does not test anyone's strength or rank players by it. It does not train a duel for a ball in the air; that waits for the heading pages and their load lines.

## Levels

- **Level 1:** as in the quick start, with D at half effort. A finds the low base and keeps the ball on the far foot.
- **Level 2:** D at full effort, still fair. A holds position under real pressure for the whole bout.
- **Level 3:** two gates about 2 m wide, one in the middle of each of two opposite sides of the square (4 more cones per pair). A scores by holding off D and then playing or dribbling out through either gate. A chooses the gate from where D is; D tries to win the ball or block the gate.
- **Level 4:** D may start from any side, chosen each bout without saying. A reads where D comes from before the first contact and turns the body to meet it. D still challenges from the side, never from behind.
- **Level 5 (needs three):** the third player serves the ball in from about 8 m outside the square, and D challenges as it arrives. A takes the first touch away from D and then shields or plays out, whichever the picture allows. Two or three sets, the same rest as before.

## For a coach

**Watch for:**

- **The base.** Is A low and wide before D arrives, or upright and easy to move?
- **The ball.** On the foot further from D, or between the feet where D can reach it?
- **The look.** Does A know where D is before the contact, or find out by being bumped?
- **The way out (Level 3 onward).** Does the gate change with where D is, or is it the same gate every time?

**One question, between bouts:** "Where was the challenger when you chose your way out?"

**During a bout:** stay quiet while the ball is moving. When a player is stuck, a question or a short cue may help ("where is the ball, near foot or far?"), never which gate or when to turn.

**Praise** the position and the choice, whether the ball was kept or not: "You got low before the contact and turned away from the side the challenge came from." A shielder who was beaten after holding a good position has done most of the work.

**Leave for later:** the arm. First the base and the body between ball and opponent; the arm comes once the base holds.

**When to simplify:** if A loses the ball on almost every bout, D challenges at half effort again, or the bout is shortened.

**Pairing:** if one player wins every bout in a pair, change the pairs; a contest lost every time teaches little.

## Why we think it helps

In a game, a player who cannot hold off a challenge loses the ball whatever the feet can do. This drill puts the strength work inside that moment, in short full-effort bouts with real rest, so the position and the ball are learned together. The body weight and the challenge are the load on this page; strength work with equipment is a separate, supervised programme, not this drill. If a player holds the ball well here and still loses it in games, play the same contest inside a small-sided game, where the challenge comes from a side the player has not seen.

## Self-assessment

- "When you kept the ball, what was your body doing before the challenge came?"
- "Which side did the challenge come from most, and how did you know?"
- "After holding them off, how did you choose your way out?"
