For a player of 9 to 16 with one helper (a parent, a friend, a coach), or up to four taking turns. This is the [Weak-Foot Dribbling Gauntlet](/training/weak-foot-dribbling-gauntlet) with one change: a card at the far end shows which gate is shut this run. The player can only find out by looking up while the ball is moving, and then has to choose a way through the gates that are still open.

## Quick start

**You need:** a ball, 21 markers, five cards numbered 1 to 5, something to number the gates (a card, a chalk mark or a shoe beside each), and flat ground about 25 m long and 9 m wide, with room or a wall behind the finish gate.

**Set it out** as the base page does:

```
          [finish gate]   2 markers, about 6 m beyond the box
                |
          [turn box]      4 markers, about 3 m square, about 2 m beyond the maze
                |
          [gate maze]     5 gates of 2 markers, about 1.5 m apart, numbered 1 to 5,
                |         scattered over about 8 m x 8 m, turned different ways
          [slalom]        5 markers, about 1 m apart, the first about 2 m from the start
          [start]
```

- 21 markers in all. Every size is a suggestion; the base page has a smaller layout for less space.
- **The helper** stands a few metres to one side of the finish gate, never beyond or behind it, facing the player, with the five cards. They never step into the course.
- **The cards** carry a number large enough to read from the start of the maze. If it is not, the helper stands nearer, beside the turn box.

**One run:**

1. Before the run, the helper picks one card without showing it.
2. **Slalom:** weave through the five markers with the weaker foot. As the player reaches the last slalom marker, the helper holds the card up at head height and keeps it there until the player leaves the maze.
3. **Gate maze:** the gate with that number is shut. Take the ball through any three of the other four gates, choosing as you go.
4. **Turn box:** go in, make one turn and come out through a different side.
5. **Finish:** roll or drive the ball through the finish gate, then walk back with it.
6. **Two numbers:** the player says how many touches the stronger foot took (the rescue touches) and whether they went through the shut gate. The helper says yes or no to the second.

**The one rule:** every touch with the weaker foot. A stronger-foot touch is allowed when the ball would otherwise be lost; it is counted, never punished.

**The first block:** 5 runs, then about 90 seconds easy; up to three blocks (see the load lines). The helper picks a new card for every run. These numbers are a suggestion.

**Load:**

- The runs are short and at a pace the player controls; nobody does extra runs as a punishment, and no run is timed against anyone else.
- For 9 to 12: two or three blocks, ending while they still want more. For 13 to 16: three blocks or fewer. Allow about 25 minutes the first time, as on the base page.
- Stop at once on pain, illness, dizziness or a movement that is breaking down; nobody plays through it, and nobody is pushed to.

**One thing to think about:** where does your head come up, and does the ball keep moving when it does?

**Easier or harder:** easier, the card goes up a little earlier (from the third slalom marker) and stays up until the turn box. Harder, see the levels below.

## What this drill is, and what it is not

This is a dribbling drill with a reading task in it. The card is a rule of the game, not an instruction: it says which gate is shut, never which way to go. The player has to look up to find it, keep the ball moving while they look, and then choose a route through what is left. In a game the same look finds where the space is closed.

It does not train beating a defender, and it is not a reaction test: nobody calls out, and the card does not change at the last moment (except at Level 5, where it changes once, in plain view).

Alone, use the base page; this one needs a helper.

## Levels

- **Level 1:** the layout stays the same for all three blocks.
- **Level 2:** move the gates between blocks, so no route can be learned by heart.
- **Level 3:** the helper holds up two cards: two gates are shut, and the player takes the ball through the other three.
- **Level 4 (different, not harder):** if there is a second ball (a futsal ball, or a ball with a little air let out), swap balls between blocks.
- **Level 5:** once in each run, while the player is in the maze, the helper swaps the card for another without a word. The newly shut gate is shut from then on, and the first gate opens again; a gate already used still counts.

## Coach guidance

**Watch for:**

- Where the head comes up. Players tend to stop the ball to read the card at first; the glance gets shorter over the sessions.
- Whether the route is chosen or followed: a player who takes the same three gates every time the card allows it has stopped reading the maze.
- The two numbers over three blocks. Both may rise at first; that is the task being new, not a problem.
- The player who looks up and loses the ball, then stops looking up. Easier first (the card up earlier), not a reminder.

**During a run:** stay quiet. The helper holds the card and says nothing.

**Between runs, one question at most:** "When did you see the number?" "Which gate did you leave out, and why that one?" "Where could you look up without losing the ball?" Never which gate, which turn or when to look.

**What not to do:** call the number out, point at the shut gate, time the runs, or compare one player's numbers with another's.

**Praise** the look and the choice: "You saw it before the maze." "You found a new way round." "The ball kept moving when you looked up."

**Leave for later:** which turn in the box, which part of the foot, speed.

**If the player is stuck** (the shut gate taken again and again): make it easier, not louder. The card up from the third slalom marker, or Level 1 for a block.

## Why we think it helps

In a game a player carrying the ball has to look up to find out what is open, and has to keep the ball while doing it. The base gauntlet trains the weaker foot through a maze the player can see by looking down. Here part of the information sits at the far end, so the head has to come up, and what it finds changes the route. We think a player who has found information while carrying, many times, is more ready to do it with a defender in front. We train the weak foot with deliberate practice, because a player who can use either foot has more options. If the player reads the card well here and still carries with the head down in games, try small games with gates to dribble through, where a gate with a defender standing in it is shut, and see whether the look is still there.

## Self-assessment

After a session, the player can ask:

- Did I see the card earlier at the end than at the start?
- Did my rescue touches go up when I looked up, and did they come back down?
- Which gate did I use least today, and why?
