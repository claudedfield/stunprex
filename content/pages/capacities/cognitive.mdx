A fourteen-year-old midfielder receives with a defender two strides away. In the second before the ball arrives there is a choice to make: turn, play it back, or hold it and wait for help. Now imagine the coach shouts "turn!" while the ball is travelling. The touch may look exactly the same. But the choice was the coach's, not the player's.

The cognitive family is the name we use for the choosing. It sits between what the player has taken in and what their body then does. The [perceptual page](/capacities/perceptual) covers the taking in. We separate the two because a coach can plan for one at a time; in a game they are one action.

## What a rep shows, and what it does not

Back to the midfielder. The touch runs long and the defender takes it. The easy sentence from the sideline is "they did not decide in time".

It might be true. It might also be that the pass came harder than it looked, that the player chose the space and mishit the touch, that the space closed while the ball was travelling, or that they had decided to protect it and the ball bounced. A heavy touch has more than one story, and each story points to different work. One mistake does not tell us much about how a player thinks, and it tells us nothing about who they will be.

The same goes for a pause. A player who takes a beat longer and then commits may be choosing well. A player who moves at once may not be choosing at all. The only person who knows what they weighed is the player, so ask them afterwards before you decide what it was.

## Who makes the choice in this rep

Take a simple receiving drill: a cone on either side, a pass on the way, a look over each shoulder while the ball travels, and a first touch towards one of the cones. If nobody says which cone, the player chooses. The choice is small, but it is theirs.

Now run the same drill with the coach calling the cone while the ball travels. That is a different drill. It practises reacting to a signal with the body ready for either side, which is useful: the player arrives open and moves late. But the decision has been made by the voice. If we call every cone on every rep, we are running a reaction drill and calling it decision training, and the players learn to wait for us.

So the question for any rep is simple: who decided? If it was the coach or a card, it can still be good practice. Just call it what it is, and make sure the choice comes back to the player somewhere in the session.

## Giving the choice back, for Tuesday

Pick one drill you already run. Find the moment where an adult decides: the called side, the "now!", the instruction before each rep about where the ball should go. Take that one thing away and put two real options in front of the player instead, so that what they see decides which one is better.

Then watch. If they find answers, even untidy ones, leave them with it for a while before you make it harder. If they are stuck, a question or a short cue that does not name the answer ("what did you see?", "watch their body") and another try. Afterwards, ask what they saw and what they chose, and listen without correcting.

Choice also adds load, and that cuts both ways. Adding a decision to a technical drill turns touches into practice for the game. Adding it before the player can control the ball in simple conditions uses up everything they have, and they practise struggling. Take the decision back out if the touch breaks down rep after rep, if the player only ever picks the safe option, or if the same failure repeats with nothing new tried in between. Harder is not always better.

## Drills in the library that keep the choice with the player

Two pages in the library do this today in their reworked form. More will follow as the other drill pages are rewritten, and we would rather show two than point you to pages that make the choice for the player.

[The Commit Window](/training/commit-window-1v1), from 13. A 1v1 in a lane: the attacker watches the defender's weight and feet and goes the moment the defender is off balance. Nobody calls the moment. It practises when to go; it leaves out teammates, a second defender in its base form, and what happens after the ball is lost.

[Self-Call Sea](/training/self-call-sea), for a player from 9 to 16 practising alone. The player turns a card mid-dribble and leaves the cone field through the gate it names; the route and the touches are theirs. It practises close control while part of the attention is elsewhere. The card is not something a player looks for in a game, so it does not practise reading opponents or teammates.

Neither drill shows how well a player decides in games. That is something you see in games, by watching the same player in the situations the drill was for, over several weeks.
