Two players do the same receiving drill, and both turn their heads before the ball arrives. The first turns because the drill says so; nothing behind them ever changes, and the touch would be the same without the look. The second turns because a defender might be on either side, and the touch has to go away from them. From the sideline the two looks are identical. Only the second one is telling the player something they need.

The perceptual family is our name for that gathering: what a player sees, hears and feels of space, teammates, opponents and the ball before and while they act. It is the front half of every action. The choosing that follows is on the [cognitive page](/capacities/cognitive).

Peripheral vision is often mixed up with looking around. It is what the eye takes in around its point of focus, and a player cannot be coached to have more of it. Looking around is something a player does, so it can be practised; "have a look before it comes" asks for something they control.

## What a look shows, and what it does not

From the side we can see a head turn. We cannot see what the player saw, or what they made of it. A player who looks calm and plays the simple pass may have seen everything and chosen it, or seen nothing and played safe. A player who hesitates may be short of information, weighing two good options, or tired.

So a head turn is worth noticing, and it is not a score. Counting looks can help a player see their own habit, if they want to; it says nothing about anybody else, and it is not a verdict on a child. Looking around is a habit, and habits are practised. We know of no age at which it is too late to start.

## Making the look matter

Take a simple receiving drill: a cone on either side, a pass on the way, a look over each shoulder while it travels, a first touch towards one of the cones. In its first versions the player chooses the cone, or the coach calls it. Either way there is nothing behind the player to find, so the look is practised as a movement and a timing. That is useful supporting work, and worth calling what it is.

Then put a defender behind the receiver who steps to one side while the ball travels. Now a touch towards the defender can go wrong, and the look decides the touch. That is the practice: what the player sees changes what they do.

For Tuesday, pick one drill where the look is asked for but has nothing to find, and add one thing that moves behind or beside the player: a defender, a partner who steps left or right, a second ball. Keep everything else the same.

## Let them look, and miss things

If we keep telling players where to look and which side is free, we take away the chance to practise looking. So let them look, and let them miss things. Afterwards, ask what they saw.

If they find the free side, leave them with it, and make it a little harder later. If they are stuck, a question or a short cue that does not name the side ("what was behind you?", "have a look before it comes") and another try.

Watch the load too. A player who is overloaded stops looking, which is the opposite of what we want. If the touch is breaking down rep after rep, or the head has stopped coming up because all the attention has gone to the ball, take a layer away.

Free play does some of this work without any of us: opponents nobody planned, uneven numbers, nobody prompting. It is worth protecting for that alone.

## What the research says, at the strength it has

In studies of professional midfielders, and in some studies of older youth players, those who looked around more in the moments before receiving tended to do better with the ball on the next action. That is an association, and a modest one; it does not show that the looking caused the better play. Studies with young players suggest simple instruction and practice can increase how often they look; how long that lasts, and whether games get better because of it, is still open. A precise number about how often top players look is often retold. It is a simplification, and it is not a target for children.

## Drills in the library where the look matters

[Wall Scan-Receive](/training/wall-scan-receive), alone with a wall, from 9. It practises the timing of the look while the ball comes back, with both feet. The markers do not move, so there is nothing new to find, except in its last variation, where a helper steps to one side and the touch goes away from them.

[The Commit Window](/training/commit-window-1v1), from 13. A 1v1 in a lane: the attacker watches the defender's hips and feet and goes when the weight is wrong. The look decides the moment.

[Harbour Run](/training/harbour-run), for 5 to 8. A sailing game through a sea of cones; when the storm is called, the child has to know where the harbour is, so the look has already happened. No adult has to say "head up".

None of these shows that a player reads games better. That is something you see in games, for the same player, over time: does the head come up before the ball arrives, and does the touch go towards space they could have seen?
