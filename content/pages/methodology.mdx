Your child has started playing in a team, they love it, and you want to do the right thing by them without knowing yet what that is. Most of it is already happening: they play, they enjoy it, and they want to go back. We think a young player develops over many years, mostly by playing, and that a child who keeps loving the game has what the rest is built on. So there is no hurry this season.

## Your part this season

Three things are yours.

**Enjoyment.** If they come home happy and want to play again, the season is going well, whatever the score was. The drive home can be about anything.

**The week.** Sleep, food, another sport if they like one, and time with a ball and nobody organising it. Team training is a few hours. The rest of the week is yours and theirs.

**The game.** Pick a few moments when the ball comes to your child and follow what they do next. Enjoy the good ones.

Some things are not yours. You do not have to coach, from the sideline or at home. You do not have to work out whether your child is talented, or which position is theirs. We do not think anyone can know either of those yet, and the reasons are below.

The rest of this page is what we believe, in thirteen short parts, so that you can decide whether it is thinking you want around your child.

## What we believe

**1. Greatness is earned.** When a fourteen-year-old looks gifted, we read it as years of play and practice that nobody was watching. Nobody knows how much of a player's game is inherited, and we will not invent a share. What we do know is that the part a player can work on is large, and it is the part they control. So we would sooner help your child do that work than wait to see who has the gift.

**2. Football is a complex, adaptive game.** In any ten seconds of a match the ball, the teammate, the opponent, the space and the score all change at once, and the player has to find an answer that did not exist a second ago. If a player has only learned a list of moves, the unfamiliar catches them out. If they have learned to read the game and adapt, they take that with them to any pitch. So we do not teach football as moves to memorise.

**3. Develop the whole player.** A child who strikes the ball well but does not look up is held back by the part that was left behind. Technique, decisions, the body, communication, the mind and the life around the game grow together. So our drills train several of these at once, and the week around the game trains the rest.

**4. Constraints guide learning.** If we tell a player where to look, when to turn and what pass to make, we take away the chance to practise exactly those things. So we prefer to set the problem and let the player find the answer: a touch limit, a smaller space, a rule that changes mid-game. If they find the answer, we leave them with it. If they do not, a question or a short cue, and another go.

**5. Development is individual.** Two children of the same age can be years apart in their bodies, their attention and what they need next, and the same child changes from one season to the next. So we compare a player only with their own earlier self. And we hand responsibility to the player as they can carry it: for their kit, their recovery, their choices, and in time for their own development.

**6. Develop the player, not the position.** If your child is put in defence this month, that is one place to learn the game from. We are not against positions. We are against a position becoming a child's identity before they have played from several places. Through sixteen we ask for rotation, across a season and within games, so that a player who arrives at a position already has the rest of the game in them. The goalkeeper is the exception, and even the keeper trains as a complete footballer first.

**7. Football is learned through football.** The kickabout in the park, the game in the street, the small game with a rule changed: we think that is where much of a player's game is built. So free play, the lift to the park and time alone with a ball matter as much to us as the organised session, and we try to make every drill look as much like the game as it can.

**8. Development extends beyond team training.** Team sessions are a few hours a week. Sleep, food, a second sport, free play and the drive home are the rest, and they are training whether anyone calls them that or not. The coach sees the sessions. The week is seen only by the people who live in it, and that is where your part is. It is a big part.

**9. Perception, decision, action.** Before the ball arrives, a player looks. With what they saw, they choose. Then they act. We train all three, and we judge a touch or a pass by what the player was trying to do as well as by whether it came off. So if your child tries a pass through the defence and it fails, we ask what they saw. If the idea was a good one, we value it more than a safe pass backwards that was not.

**10. Variability, adaptability and resilience.** A skill that works in one drill, on one surface, against one defender, often goes missing in a match. So we vary the conditions on purpose, make training harder and denser than the game and then adjust it to the player. How hard is the coach's to judge, player by player, and too much is to be avoided. For you it means that a session which looks difficult is not a bad sign in itself. And we treat a setback as something a player can learn to get through, with help. It does not tell us who they are.

**11. Mistakes are part of the path.** A lost ball, a heavy touch and a pass that did not come off each tell the player something, if they are allowed to look at it. If a player is punished for a mistake, they learn to hide the next one, and a hidden mistake teaches them little. So we would rather a player tried the harder option and failed than played safe to avoid being wrong, and we say so to the players and to the sideline.

**12. Long-term development over short-term success.** The score tells you how one game went. What a player did on purpose, the look, the touch, the decision, the pass with the weaker foot, is what they can repeat, and we think later results are built on that. A result at ten, a selection at twelve, a trial at fourteen: each is one view of a player who is still changing. So we think about the adult player more than about Saturday.

**13. Joy is not optional.** If your child has stopped enjoying the game, that is the first thing we attend to, before any drill. Pressure can wear down a player's enjoyment and the quality of their play before anything visible breaks. Children keep developing because they keep playing, and they keep playing because they love it.

## How we describe a player

We describe a player through six families of capacity. They are a way of organising what can be trained, and they are not a score. Every drill is tagged with the ones it works.

- [Perceptual](/capacities/perceptual): what the player takes in.
- [Cognitive](/capacities/cognitive): what they do with it.
- [Motor](/capacities/motor): what they can do with the ball and the body.
- [Communication](/capacities/communication): how they coordinate with others.
- [Affective](/capacities/affective): how they feel and keep going.
- [Adaptive](/capacities/adaptive): how they handle change.

## What we will not do

We will not rank, label or score your child, frighten you into acting now, invent a number, or put the basics behind a price.

## What is on the site

**The drills.** A free library at [the training page](/training). You do not need them to do your part.

**The games.** [Free browser games](/games) built around looking and deciding. We have not tested whether a score in a game carries over to a pitch, so we do not claim it.

**The blog and the newsletter.** Longer pieces on [the blog](/blog), and one short piece every two weeks by email, written for parents first. Nothing in them asks you to coach at home.

You can ask a question at [the community](/community).

**If you are a player:** start with [Weak-Foot Dribbling Gauntlet](/training/weak-foot-dribbling-gauntlet) or [The Commit Window](/training/commit-window-1v1).

**If you are a coach:** take one drill you already run and remove one instruction about direction, so the players have to look to know.
