## What we are for

StunpreX exists for one thing: to help the people around a young footballer serve that player well, over the years it takes, without the routine harms of youth football. The people are the parent, the player, and the coach, in that order of who we write for first. The player is a whole person, not a set of statistics and not a position, and the horizon is long enough that this season's results are a small part of the story.

Everything on this site follows from a set of positions about how a player develops. This page is those positions in plain words, what we will not do because of them, and how the drills, the newsletter and the rest are built on them.

## What we believe

**Development is trained.** What looks like a gift in a fourteen-year-old is, in our reading, years of work that nobody was watching, play included. We do not know how much of anyone's game is inherited and we will not invent a share; what we can say is that the trained part is large, it is the part a player controls, and it is the part we work on.

**The horizon is long.** A result at ten, a selection at twelve, a trial at fourteen: each is a snapshot of a player who is still changing. We hold that junior results and selections are a weak guide to the player someone becomes, and we write and coach for the player at twenty-two, not for Saturday.

**Process before outcome.** The score records what was countable today. What a player did, the look before the ball arrived, the first touch, the choice under pressure, the week around the game, is what they can repeat on Tuesday, and it is what we help parents and coaches notice instead. A count of those things describes a behaviour for one player over time; it is never a score, a norm, or a comparison with another child.

**The whole player.** Technique, decisions, the body, communication, the mind and the life around the work all constrain one another. A player who trains one of them at the expense of the rest is not developing; they are unbalancing. Sleep, food, free play and other sports are part of training, not the gap between sessions.

**The player owns it.** Coaches and parents are supporting cast. Responsibility for the work passes to the player gradually, as they can carry it, and a programme that never passes it has stopped developing a player and started managing one. Self-assessment is taught, not assumed.

**The coach sets problems.** An answer the coach supplies is remembered; an answer the player finds is theirs, and it is the one that appears in a game where nobody supplies anything. So our drills are built so that the behaviour we want is the way through the task, and in the development years the coach's voice, like a teammate's, stays quiet enough for the player to do the seeing and the deciding.

**Failure is information.** A lost ball, a failed through-ball, a heavy touch each contain a lesson, and whether the lesson is taken depends on the environment. A player who is punished for a mistake learns to hide the next one, and a hidden mistake cannot be learned from. We would rather a player attempted the harder option and lost the ball than learned to protect a completion rate.

**Joy is not optional.** Pressure taxes joy and quality before anything visible breaks, and a player who has stopped enjoying the game has stopped developing in it. We watch for that before we watch for a technical fault.

## The six capacities

We describe a player through six families of capacity. They are a way of organising what can be trained, not a map of the brain and not a score; every drill in the library is tagged with the families it works, and every game expresses several at once.

**Perceptual: what the player takes in.** Everything a player gathers about the situation, by looking, listening, feeling the ball and the ground, and working out what is likely next, gathered on purpose rather than received by chance. The look before the ball arrives is the moment of it a parent can see from the sideline; the whole of it runs through the game, in and out of possession. [The perceptual capacity](/capacities/perceptual).

**Cognitive: what the player does with it.** Choosing an action when there is more than one and little time; holding what was just seen long enough to use it; keeping attention on what matters; anticipating a pass or two ahead; holding back the obvious response when a better one exists; and noticing your own thinking. We think this is the part of a player's game that keeps growing longest, and that it is taught and practised rather than absorbed by playing years. [The cognitive capacity](/capacities/cognitive).

**Motor: what the player can do.** Ball mastery on both feet, coordination, balance, speed and its changes, agility, power, endurance, mobility, and the techniques of passing, receiving, striking and defending. Assessed in real conditions, not for textbook form. Of everything a player builds, we think the relationship with the ball outlasts the rest, and we protect daily time with it. [The motor capacity](/capacities/motor).

**Communication: how the player coordinates with others.** Calls, listening through the noise, signals, reading a teammate's body, the timing of collective movement. Trained deliberately, in designs built for it, and layered in as the player's own reading matures; in the development years the default is quiet, because one child's voice can do another child's seeing. [The communication capacity](/capacities/communication).

**Affective: how the player feels and sustains.** Composure when it matters, the reset after a mistake, confidence built from a record of things done rather than from affirmation, focus, motivation that survives a setback, resilience, discipline, and the love of the game underneath all of it. Observed over time and with the player's own account, never diagnosed from the sideline. [The affective capacity](/capacities/affective).

**Adaptive: how the player handles change.** Surprise, a new rule, a new surface, a new coach, a scoreline that flips; the lesson taken from a failure; skills that hold when the conditions do not. Built through varied but purposeful demands, not variety for its own sake. [The adaptive capacity](/capacities/adaptive).

## What we will not do

Because of the above, there are things this site will not do, whatever the traffic or the market says.

We will not rank, score or benchmark a young player in public, and we will not publish a norm that tells a parent what a child of that age "should" be doing. A count on this site is for one player against their own earlier self.

We will not label a child talented or not, and we will not write as if a player's ceiling can be read at ten. We keep positions open through sixteen: we will not lock a player into one before then, or sell specialisation as seriousness.

We will not frighten parents. There is no version of this site in which a child's future depends on acting now, and no countdown on anything.

We will not invent a number. A rate, a percentage, a timeline or a target that we cannot source is not on the page; where the evidence is thin we say so in words.

We will not promise a pathway, a selection, a scholarship or a transfer, and we will not present a testimonial, a badge or a proprietary score as evidence that a player is developing.

We will not put the basics behind a price. The drills, the games and the newsletter are free and stay free; anything we sell is an instrument on top of them, never the method itself.

## How the site follows from it

**The drills.** A free library of drills, each tagged with the capacities it works and written to a standard: the game problem first, then the setup, what the task practises, what it does not establish, and how to check its usefulness in play. Three to start from, one for each of three habits we keep returning to: [Scan-Decide-Receive](/training/scan-decide-receive-two-cone), the look before the ball arrives; [Directional First Touch](/training/directional-first-touch), the touch that keeps the next action open; [Position-Rotation SSG](/training/position-rotation-ssg), every player in every role. The full library is at [the training page](/training).

**The games.** A set of free browser games built around perception and decision. They are enjoyable and they exercise something; we make no claim that a score in a game transfers to a pitch, because we have not tested that and the honest position is that we do not know.

**The blog and the newsletter.** Long pieces on the site, and one focused piece a week by email, written for parents first: what to notice at a game instead of the score, what a first touch makes possible, why a failed pass can still be the right choice, the week around the game. Nothing in them asks a parent to coach at home.

**The community answers.** Real questions from parents, players and coaches, answered in the same voice, with the same refusals.

**What is coming.** A training journal for the player is in production. Drill packs, longer programmes, a membership built around a record of demonstrated development that stays private to the player and their family, and coach education are planned on the same principles. None of them is promised here before it exists, and none of them will change what is free.

## What you can do this week

If you are a parent: at the next game, follow a few receptions through to the action after them, and enjoy the good ones. The drive home can be about anything. [The blog](/blog) has the rest.

If you are a player: pick one of the three drills above and run it easy enough that the ball stays with you, then a little harder when it does. Afterwards, ask yourself what you saw, what you chose and what happened, and keep the answers to yourself unless you want to share them.

If you are a coach: take one drill you already run and remove one instruction about direction, so the players have to look to know. Then say nothing during it except the rules. [The community](/community) is where to bring what happened.
