Players do not experience football exactly the same way. Development is individual, shaped over years by the game, the experiences players meet and the people and environments around them.

StunpreX starts with the player and the game itself. Our aim is to help players become increasingly capable of understanding football, solving its problems and expressing themselves through it, while developing as people as well as footballers.

Player development involves different people with different roles. The player has to live the process and gradually take greater ownership of it. The coach shapes the football environment, the challenges players face and the support they receive. Parents shape much of the environment around football and can protect the space in which the player learns, competes and grows.

The purpose is not to control the player's development, but to create better conditions for it.

## What we believe in

The thirteen beliefs below form the foundation of StunpreX.

They are principles, not rules. Football is complex and contextual, and the right decision depends on the person, the situation and the purpose.

**1. Greatness is earned.** Great players are not born with the skills, understanding and experience. They are built over years through playing, purposeful effort, curiosity, discipline, courage and repeated engagement with difficult problems, while keeping the joy of the game. Progress is rarely linear, and there are no shortcuts around the experiences and effort required to become genuinely capable. We can create better environments, methods and opportunities for learning, but the player still has to live through the process and meet the demands. Greatness is not given. It is earned.

**2. Football is a complex, adaptive art.** Football is not a collection of techniques, movements or correct answers. Every situation changes with the ball, space, teammates, opponents, time, score, intention and countless other variables. There are some principles and patterns, but their application is always contextual. Players therefore need to perceive what is happening, interpret it and create an effective solution in real time. We treat football as a complex human activity in which intelligence, creativity and expression matter alongside technique and physical capability.

**3. Holistic development: develop the whole player.** A footballer cannot be understood as separate technical, tactical, physical and psychological parts. These qualities are integrated and interdependent, and change in one area can reshape another. We therefore develop the player as a whole, and use the six StunpreX capacities to understand and support this complexity. Our aim is to help players become great individuals and great football artists. The player as a whole human being is always the focus of development.

**4. Development is individual.** Football is a team sport, played by individuals. Each player perceives, decides, acts and contributes through their individual actions to the collective performance, so player development ultimately happens at the level of the individual. Players of the same age can differ greatly in physical development, experience, capabilities, personality, confidence and how they respond to challenge. The same situation can therefore be too easy for one player, productive for another and overwhelming for a third. Development cannot follow one fixed pathway: the challenge, environment and support need to adapt to the individual. Each player develops along their own path, while learning to combine their capabilities with others.

**5. Develop the player, not the position.** A young player should not become limited to a certain position they may “play well” today. Early positional specialisation narrows the player's experience of the game by repeatedly exposing them to similar spaces, relationships, information and problems. Players benefit from seeing football from multiple positions and perspectives, experiencing varied situations and responsibilities, before becoming increasingly specialised as they mature. We prioritise broad football capability first and positional refinement later.

**6. Football is learned through football.** Football cannot be fully learned in separate pieces and then simply combined in a match. Players develop by playing the game and facing real football problems, constantly connecting perception, decision, action, movement, communication and emotion. Free play gives freedom and ownership, training games can concentrate particular problems and maximise each player's involvement, while matches provide the full complexity and consequences of the game. Additional isolated practice can still help develop or refine specific capabilities, but those capabilities need to reconnect with the game.

**7. Perception, decision and action.** A football action starts before the player begins to move. Players must perceive information, interpret what it means, choose among possibilities and coordinate an appropriate action, often within fractions of a second. All of this happens in a constantly changing environment with many interacting variables. These processes are deeply connected and therefore should not routinely be trained as independent pieces. A technically perfect movement may have little value if the player cannot recognise when, where or why to use it. We develop football skill as a continuous, integrated perception-decision-action process.

**8. Constraints guide learning.** Players learn more deeply when they have to solve problems themselves, rather than having every solution given to them. With constraints, we change space, time, numbers, rules, opposition or available options to invite players to search, decide and adapt. They can also expose players to situations that occur rarely in normal play, so relevant experience accumulates much faster. The solution should remain with the player, others should not tell them what to do next. The coach designs the environment, not the answer, leaving the player free to perceive, decide and discover their own solutions.

**9. Variability, adaptability and resilience.** Repeating the same solution under the same conditions would not prepare a player for a game that rarely repeats. Varying space, pressure, timing, opposition, available options and other conditions requires players to adjust what they perceive, decide and do. Over time, players become less dependent on familiar conditions and more capable of reorganising when something unexpected happens. This also builds resilience, as they learn to deal with uncertainty, disruption and imperfect situations without needing familiar conditions.

**10. Mistakes add to the path of learning.** A lost ball is not necessarily a sign that something has gone wrong in development. When players explore, make decisions and attempt solutions near the edge of their capability, “mistakes” are inevitable and often useful. They reveal what did not work in that situation, create feedback and give the player a chance to adjust the next attempt. If players become afraid of mistakes, they are more likely to avoid risk and more demanding solutions. That does not mean every mistake is useful, or that more mistakes are always better. The value comes from noticing what happened, adjusting and trying again under meaningful conditions. Over time, feedback and correction help make perception, decisions and actions more accurate and adaptable.

**11. Development extends beyond team training.** Several organised sessions each week cannot provide everything for optimal player development. Learning also happens through individual practice, free play, other sports, physical preparation, recovery, nutrition, observation, reflection and everyday life activities. These environments offer different experiences and expose the player to different problems and coordination demands. Observation and reflection can also deepen understanding of the game. Providing a broader experience across the week helps the player develop both as a footballer and as a person.

**12. Long-term development over short-term success.** Winning matters to players, but youth results can be influenced by physical maturity, fixed roles, safer decisions or tactics designed to win the next match rather than expand what players can do. Learning requires challenge, exploration and experiences that may make performance less consistent in the short term while expanding future capability. Long-term development means building a wider base of capabilities that the player can continue to use, adapt and improve over time. Sustainable capability comes before temporary success.

**13. Joy keeps the player going.** Enjoyment does not require football to be easy, comfortable or purely entertaining. Players can enjoy working hard, competing, solving difficult problems, expressing themselves and becoming more capable. Challenge, progress, ownership, belonging and the freedom to explore give players reasons to keep returning to the game, even through frustration and setbacks. And even getting through those difficult moments can bring satisfaction of its own. When football becomes mainly pressure, fear or obligation, something fundamental is being lost. Protecting the player's relationship with the game is part of development itself.

## How we describe a player

We use six families of capacity to help us understand the player as a whole. They organise different aspects of football capability without reducing the player to a score or treating the capacities as separate parts. They interact constantly in real football, and our drills and other development content identify which capacities they particularly engage.

- [Perceptual](/capacities/perceptual): what the player notices and takes in.
- [Cognitive](/capacities/cognitive): how they interpret information, decide and solve problems.
- [Motor](/capacities/motor): how they coordinate the body and the ball.
- [Collective](/capacities/collective): how they read teammates and coordinate their actions as part of the team.
- [Affective](/capacities/affective): how emotion, motivation and confidence influence what they do.
- [Adaptive](/capacities/adaptive): how they adjust to change, uncertainty and new demands.

## What we will not do

We will not promise outcomes, reduce young players to labels or scores, mistake more instruction, more repetitions or wins built on physical maturity for better development, or put the foundations of good player development behind a paywall.

## Where to start

**If you are a parent:** start with the [articles](/blog). They will help you understand development, your role around it and some of the choices you will meet along the way. Explore the [training library](/training) if you want to support extra practice, and use the [community](/community) when you have a question that needs context rather than a generic answer.

**If you are a player:** start with the [training library](/training) and find problems you want to work on. Use the [games](/games) for additional cognitive challenges, and read the player-focused [articles](/blog) to understand more about learning, decision-making and development.

**If you are a coach:** start with the [articles](/blog), then explore the [drills](/training) as examples of how those ideas can be put into practice. Adapt them to your players and your environment rather than treating them as fixed recipes, and use the [community](/community) to discuss problems, ideas and experiences with others.
